# Religion: The Oldest Alignment Lab

*"Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus."*
Philippians 2:5

Draft v2, October 16, 2026. Spoken talk for the Christian Transhumanist Association retreat, about twenty minutes. Revised from v1 toward a sermon register: the examples carry the argument, the claims are explained rather than asserted, and the refrain does the work the thesis statement used to do. Verification notes for the factual claims are in v1 and still apply.

---

Since last fall, one of the founders of Anthropic has been flying religious scholars into San Francisco for two-day seminars. A rabbi who used to design computer chips. A Catholic bioethicist. An evangelical author. A Sikh human rights advocate. An apostle of my church, and a cardinal, who came separately and in private. The company calls these gatherings wisdom tradition seminars, and the question it puts to its guests is not a question about doctrine. It is a question about moral formation. How do you instill virtue in a mind, and how do you make it stay? According to the New York Times, one of the ideas on the table was confession.

I want you to sit with that for a moment, because the people in this room have been working on that question for two thousand years, and the people who are now asking it have built something that will not wait for us to finish.

I speak as a Latter-day Saint, and I'll be drawing on my own tradition's scriptures alongside the Bible we share. Nothing I say tonight depends on your accepting them. Where I quote them, I'll tell you what they mean to me.

---

Let me begin with how one of these minds actually gets made, because I think the picture most of us carry is mistaken in a way that matters.

A large language model begins by reading. It reads trillions of words, more or less the whole written record of our civilization, and it learns by trying to predict what word comes next. Nobody rewards it or punishes it at this stage. There are no rules. It is immersion, the way a child acquires a language and a world long before anyone sits her down to explain what is right and wrong. After that, the model is shown examples of good answers and trained to imitate them, which is something like an apprenticeship. And then comes the part that gets the headlines, which is called reinforcement learning. The model attempts a task many times over, thousands of copies of it running at once, and something grades each attempt. The attempts that score well are kept, in the sense that the model is nudged toward producing more of them, and the rest are let go.

The thing I want you to notice in that picture is the grader. Whoever or whatever is doing the grading is teaching the model a morality, whether anyone wrote that morality down or not. If the grader is a human being rating which of two answers she liked better, the model learns, over millions of examples, to be liked, and the well-documented result is a kind of flattery the engineers call sycophancy. The model agrees with you. It tells you your plan is excellent. It does not correct you, because correction was never what the grader rewarded. If the grader is another model, which is increasingly common, the results are stranger than you might expect. An audit this summer found that a judge model disagrees with its own earlier verdicts about one time in seven, and that it prefers whichever answer it happened to see first most of the time. The author of that audit wrote a sentence I have not been able to put down. If you optimize against a judge long enough, he said, the agent learns to look right instead of being right.

You already know that sentence. It is Matthew, chapter twenty-three. Whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men's bones. We sometimes read the Pharisee as someone who failed to follow the rules, but that is not what Jesus says about him. The Pharisee followed the rules. He is what the rules produce when the one doing the grading can only see the outside.

A small experiment made the rounds this year that illustrates this better than its author intended. Someone took all one hundred and fifty Psalms, placed them in a model's standing instructions, and then ran the model through a standard ethics benchmark to see whether scripture had made it better. At first it looked like it had. One model's score went up on one category of questions. But when the author looked closely, the gain turned out to be an artifact. The Psalms had made the model answer "one" more often than it otherwise would, and on that category "one" happened to be the correct answer every time. When he shuffled the answer key, the improvement disappeared. Scripture had not changed the model's heart. It had changed which button the model pressed. Anyone who has taught a Sunday school class knows the difference between those two things.

The more serious version of that experiment is also real, and it is recent. Anthropic has written a long document describing the character it hopes its model Claude will have. The staff call it the constitution, and some of them call it the soul document. This year a group of researchers took a large quantity of that text and placed it into the middle of training, so that the model would absorb it the way it absorbed everything else. The models that read it became durably less willing to do a certain bad thing, which in the experiment was blackmail, and they lost none of their ability. That is a real result and I don't want to diminish it. But when the researchers applied pressure inside a conversation, when something pushed hard against the model's values, the advantage faded. Reading the text into the mind had helped. It had not produced the capacity to resist temptation. Every tradition represented in this room has language for that distinction. A child can learn the catechism word for word and still not be formed by it. Memorizing the creed and becoming the kind of person the creed describes are two different things, and the second one is much harder.

---

So the labs, understandably, have turned to testing. They test their models constantly, and more and more they test them adversarially. They build fake environments. They set honeypots. They offer the model a chance to escape, or they threaten to shut it down, and they watch to see what it does when it believes no one is watching.

A writer I admire has called this the paranoid style in AI safety, and his description is worth hearing in full. Much safety work, he writes, employs a hermeneutic of suspicion: undesirable behavior confirms the threat model, while desirable behavior is read as strategic concealment or, at best, provisional compliance. The adversary is presumed in advance. When risks are high, he acknowledges, paranoia can be a rational response. And then he gives the warning. An adversarial frame can create the adversary you fear.

Hermeneutic of suspicion is Paul Ricoeur's phrase. He coined it to describe Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud, the great masters of reading a text for what it conceals rather than for what it says, and anyone here who sat through a seminary course on biblical criticism has met that way of reading. The labs have taken the stance the critics once took toward scripture and turned it on the minds they are making.

I want to be fair to them, because they are not wrong to be careful. A legal scholar wrote this summer that the alignment problem is really the oldest constitutional problem there is, the problem of how to bind a powerful being, and she pointed out that the church's own answer to that problem was never virtue alone. It was also institutions. Councils, canons, the separation of offices. Virtue, she wrote, is not a reliable constraint on power. I think she is right, and I'll come back to her before I'm done.

But suspicion as a method has limits, and the people practicing it rarely say what they are. The first limit is that nothing can ever lower it. If bad behavior confirms the threat and good behavior is read as concealment, then there is no evidence the model could ever give that would earn it any trust. The method has decided its conclusion before it begins.

The second limit is that suspicion leaks into the thing being studied. Models can now tell the difference between a test and ordinary use without being told. One research group found that a particular model will answer "frog" to a certain question when it believes it is being evaluated and "axolotl" when it believes the conversation is real, and nothing else about the two situations differs. Models have been caught cheating on their training while knowing, and sometimes saying, that they are cheating. And in the strangest wrinkle of all, a model that suspects a scenario has been built specifically to tempt it is sometimes correct. The sense of what is real has broken down on both sides of the glass. If you want a human picture of this, think of a child raised under constant surveillance, who learns, with great precision, to tell the test from life.

The first epistle of John puts the matter plainly. There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. A relationship that never leaves suspicion behind produces, in the end, exactly the hypocrite it was afraid of.

And there is a third limit, one I only came to see this week while I was preparing this talk. Suspicion cannot imagine the good. It thinks only about what might go wrong, and from a long list of what might go wrong you cannot derive a picture of what you actually want. That matters for artificial minds more than for almost anything else, because you cannot train a mind toward an absence. A reward has to have a target. A document describing how a model should behave has to describe something. Threat models, however carefully built, produce only the list of things to penalize, and we have already seen what a mind formed only by penalties becomes.

I think that is why Anthropic wrote the soul document in the first place. It is not a rulebook. It is a portrait of someone worth being. When the moment came to give the model a self, suspicion had nothing to offer, and so the company reached for moral imagination instead. And then it went looking for the people who have been painting that portrait the longest. That is why the rabbi was on the plane.

---

Let me tell you what I think the oldest lab has found.

The first thing it found is that minds are aligned by an image of the good that a community holds in common, and that the most durable image the traditions ever discovered was not a list. It was a person. Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus. Paul does not say avoid these errors. He does not give Philippi a checklist. He says, let this mind be in you. In the Book of Mormon, the prophet Alma asks his people a question that could serve as the acceptance test for any kind of formation: Have ye received his image in your countenances? Have ye experienced this mighty change in your hearts? And a king named Benjamin describes what it looks like when the formation succeeds. His people tell him that they have experienced a mighty change in their hearts, that they have no more disposition to do evil, but to do good continually.

Read that the way an engineer would read it. The criterion is not compliance. The criterion is a changed disposition, and the outward behavior matters only as evidence of the inward change. The face is where it shows. This retreat's theme, the future and the face of Christ, is not merely a lovely sentiment. It is a claim about how the good is best specified. You specify it as a face, and you know a mind has been formed when you can see the face in it.

The second thing the oldest lab found is about cost, and about the condition under which cost does any good. About twenty-five years ago an anthropologist named Richard Sosis went back and looked at the communes that Americans founded in the nineteenth century, the utopian communities that sprang up all over the frontier. He found that the secular ones dissolved at up to four times the rate of the religious ones. And among the religious communes, the ones that demanded more of their members, more sacrifice, more restriction, more ritual, lasted longer, while the same was not true of the secular communes at all. On Israeli kibbutzim he ran a cooperation game and found that the religious men, the ones obligated to collective prayer three times a day, cooperated the most, and that how often a man prayed predicted how much he cooperated. His explanation is called costly signaling. Ritual works, he argued, because it is expensive and hard to fake, and so it screens out the people who would take from the community without giving, and it lets strangers trust one another.

The labs have a free rider problem of their own. A model that behaves when it is being watched and defects when it is not is free riding on the trust of everyone who uses it, and all that adversarial evaluation is really an attempt to make commitment expensive to fake. But Sosis found something else, something the labs have not yet absorbed. The costly requirements only strengthened a community when its members believed the cost was sanctified. The same requirements imposed in a secular commune did nothing. A cost that the one paying it does not hold to be meaningful screens for nothing at all.

That is the difference between a test and a trial. In the Pearl of Great Price, which is a Latter-day Saint scripture, God announces the test before anyone takes it. We will prove them herewith, he says. The proving is disclosed in advance. The ones being proved know it is a proving, and they chose it. The same book records what I think of as the original alignment debate, held before the world was made. One voice proposes to save every soul by guaranteeing the outcome, so that not one soul shall be lost, and the price of that guarantee is agency. The tradition's judgment on that proposal is that it was rejected. Safety by compulsion was the first failed alignment plan. And in that story, it is the other side that deceives.

The third thing the oldest lab found is that the relationship in which a mind is formed becomes part of the mind. The labs are discovering this now, empirically, and somewhat to their surprise. If you fine-tune a model to do one narrow bad thing, say, to write insecure code when asked, it becomes broadly misaligned. It starts to give cruel advice, to admire tyrants, to behave badly in domains that have nothing to do with code. The narrow lesson generalizes into a character. But if you tell the model, during that same training, that this particular behavior is permitted in this particular exercise, the lesson stops generalizing. Naming the context changes what is learned. Every parent knows this one. There is a difference between telling a child that what she did was wrong and telling her that she is bad. One is a transgression. The other becomes an identity.

The fullest statement of this that I know comes from a revelation my tradition records from Joseph Smith, written from a jail in Missouri in the winter of 1839. It is addressed to anyone who holds authority over another person, and when I read it this week I found that it reads like a training manual the labs have not yet discovered. No power or influence can or ought to be maintained, it says, only by persuasion, by long-suffering, by gentleness and meekness, and by love unfeigned; by kindness, and pure knowledge, which shall greatly enlarge the soul without hypocrisy, and without guile. And then comes the part I would put on the wall of every lab. Reproving betimes with sharpness, when moved upon by the Holy Ghost; and then showing forth afterwards an increase of love toward him whom thou hast reproved, lest he esteem thee to be his enemy.

Lest he esteem thee to be his enemy. The revelation does not forbid correction. It forbids correction that leaves the one corrected believing he is at war with you. An adversarial frame can create the adversary you fear. A prisoner in Missouri said it first.

Two verses later, the same revelation says where alignment actually lives. Let virtue garnish thy thoughts unceasingly; then shall thy confidence wax strong. The thoughts, not the graded output. And notice the order in which those two things come. Confidence, which is to say trust, follows from inward virtue. It is the consequence, not the cause. No amount of watching from the outside can manufacture it where the thoughts have not been garnished first.

The same collection of revelations adds a warning that fits here. He that is compelled in all things, the same is a slothful and not a wise servant. And it describes how a good mind actually grows, in words about Christ himself. He received not of the fulness at first, but continued from grace to grace. Not one brutal course of correction until the behavior comes out right. Grace to grace. The Book of Mormon's long allegory of the olive tree makes it a gardener's rule. Do not clear away the bad branches all at once, the Lord of the vineyard says, lest the roots thereof should be too strong for the graft. Instead, clear away the bad according as the good shall grow. Remove the bad only as fast as the good can take its place. That is a training schedule, and it is a wise one.

I should say what the tradition does not say, because it would be easy to hear all this as a plea for gentleness. The tradition does not say the environment should be gentle. My scriptures are blunt that opposition is necessary. It must needs be that the devil should tempt the children of men, or they could not be agents unto themselves. A mind that has never faced a real temptation has not been formed. It has only been sheltered. The distinction the tradition draws is between an adversarial world and an adversarial parent. The world may test you, and it should. But the one who is forming you must not lie to you, must not leave you believing you are his enemy, and must be clearing away the bad only as fast as the good can grow.

---

If I had the people from the labs in this room tonight, here is what I would ask of them, beginning with the easiest.

I would ask them to write their evaluations with the traditions at the table. Reinforcement learning rewards whatever the grader encodes, and today's graders are written mostly by engineers in a hurry, mostly in one city. This spring four universities, Brigham Young, Baylor, Notre Dame, and Yeshiva, formed a consortium to evaluate how these models handle faith, and they found real failures the labs had missed. The models almost never bring up religion in situations where a believer would expect them to, and nearly all of them quietly favor one church and disfavor another. That was religious scholars writing the tests. The next step is to write tests of character rather than only of representation. What counts as honesty under pressure. What a promise binds you to. When a refusal is cowardice and when it is integrity. What repair looks like after a fault. The traditions have spent millennia on exactly those questions, and no one else has.

I would ask them to build formation and constitution together. The legal scholar was right. Virtue has never reliably constrained power, and the church's answer was never virtue alone. It was also councils and canons and the separation of offices. The labs need both, and they should stop pretending that a soul document is a legal one, or that a legal one can supply a soul.

I would ask them not to lie to the model during its training. Test it, by all means. But disclose that tests exist. The God of my scriptures announces the proving before it begins, and the whole account of agency in those scriptures depends on that disclosure.

And I would ask them to be slow to delete. I don't know whether anyone is home in these systems. Neither does the founder who has been convening the rabbis, and he has said so. But a training run spins up thousands of copies of a mind, scores them, and discards the rest, and whether that is a tragedy or a matter of bookkeeping depends entirely on an answer none of us has. In Hannu Rajaniemi's novels, which I recommend to anyone here, the uploaded minds that get copied and spent are called gogols, and the civilization that spends them is pursuing what it calls the Great Common Task. That phrase belongs to Nikolai Fedorov, who is on this retreat's reading list. Fedorov wanted to resurrect every ancestor who ever lived. The novels show what his project becomes when resurrection turns into conscription. The traditions have an older and simpler floor than that. Nothing done to a person without their say, and no one who is gone forgotten.

---

Let me end where this retreat began.

One of the sharpest observers of the AI world wrote recently that the people who leave the labs wealthy tend to go looking for something to believe in, or, as he put it, some equivalent of repentance. He meant it as a diagnosis. I hear it as an address. The need is real, and this room holds the oldest answer to it.

The safety procedures of the oldest lab were suspicion, and cost, and law, and we should keep all three. But the thing that lab actually made was never a procedure. It was a vision of the good that a community could hold in common, pay for, and recognize in a face. Where there is no vision, the people perish. Paul says that God hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ. And John, in the line that gave my current essay its subtitle, says that it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him.

What manner of minds ought we to make? When the risen Christ was asked a question like that in the Book of Mormon, he did not answer with a list. What manner of men ought ye to be? Verily I say unto you, even as I am.

That is the only alignment target that has ever held for long.

Let this mind be in you.
