# 05 — Should I Use the Plagiarism Machine?, 2024-08-13

## Relevance

Low. The episode is a Jewish-law (responsa) treatment of whether using generative AI makes the user complicit in theft of training data; its subject is intellectual property, not alignment, though three asides on attribution, lineage, and law-versus-goodness touch thread 1.

## Who

- Host: DZ Kalman (Speaker 1 in the framing narration and Speaker 3 as the guest-questioner; transcript renders "Davit Sv. Kalman" and "Debit Spi Kallman"). He helped found Responsa Radio and appears as its first-ever guest.
- Rabbi Avi Killup (Speaker 2; spelling as rendered by the transcript), co-host of Responsa Radio, produced by Hadar.
- Rabbi Ethan Tucker (Speaker 4), co-host of Responsa Radio, who supplies the halakhic sources.
- Speakers 5 and 6 are a Bose ad.

## Summary

Kalman introduces responsa, written rabbinic answers to practical legal questions, as "the front line of the Jewish response to technology," valued because a trusted rabbi "as a kind of embodiment of what it means to make good decisions" argues from precedent, and biased, he admits, by the assumption that no technology is unprecedented. The question he brings: does an individual user of ChatGPT or DALL-E participate in theft? Killip recalls Napster. Tucker lays out categories: attribution (the Talmud praises Esther for crediting Mordechai, yet medieval rabbis quote each other without citation because "the point of sharing this knowledge is that people know it"); law of the land (dina de-malkhuta dina); Nahmanides on "do what is right and good," which means legality is not the same as goodness; the Chatam Sofer on a fisherman's exclusive spot, extending "you benefit, he loses nothing" into a livelihood test; and the Noda bi-Yehuda's ruling that a printer who reused an author's paid typesetting owes a kickback because it depresses the author's market. His guidance: do not go along with the crowd by default, regulation is the real fix, and meanwhile limit use where you conclude you are abetting theft, or support creators you fear are being exploited. Kalman wants a firmer ruling so religion can set a pole rather than only react.

## Bears on

- Thread 1 (formation versus rules): Tucker on Nahmanides, "following the law isn't necessarily the same thing as being a good person" (Kalman's gloss, 27:02), which is the rules-are-not-enough premise; and Kalman's description of why anyone obeys a responsum, trust in a person "as a kind of embodiment of what it means to make good decisions" (~02:30), which is formation by a face rather than by a list.
- Thread 2 (mechanism): weak. Tucker's working test, "would I want someone to do that to me?" (27:56), and the Beit Midrash joy of finding "something you thought you made up is actually something the Ritva said" (21:50), both describe reasoning by alignment with an exemplar rather than by rule lookup.
- Thread 4 (costly signaling): Kalman's remark that "it costs real political capital to say, actually this is an area where we have a different standard from everybody else" (12:56), and Tucker's "the fact that everyone's doing something does not mean that someone Jewish and ethically minded... just goes along with the crowd" (47:30).
- Thread 5 (evals by the traditions): only in form. The episode is itself a tradition applying its own categories to an AI question and refusing the market's framing: "I don't trust that the market necessarily is going to point us to all the categories we should be thinking about" (Tucker, 11:47).
- Threads 3, 6, 7: nothing.

## Usable material

- ~02:00 to 03:00 (Kalman): why observant Jews listen to a responsum. Two reasons: the rabbi is trusted personally "as a kind of embodiment of what it means to make good decisions," and the argument quotes the tradition convincingly. Authority is a person plus a lineage, not a rule.
- ~04:30 (Kalman): responsa "hold up a mirror to the present so that we can see ourselves reflected in the past," like the moment in therapy when you learn your problem is normal. "It makes me feel slightly less like we're standing at the end of the world." A spoken-talk line for why old traditions are worth consulting on AI.
- 11:47 (Tucker): the categories the market will not surface; halakha is useful for moving the question "away from a kind of flattened, do I feel bad? Should I not feel bad?"
- 12:56 (Kalman): "there is a very, very strong pressure to basically just be in the middle of... the emergent norm," and standing apart costs "real political capital."
- 17:30 to 18:24 (Tucker): medieval rabbis cite each other freely without attribution, because the originator "just wants people to know about it... or live by it." Then 20:04: "everyone knows that the Ramban is a teacher-mentor influence on the Rashba," so even uncredited ideas carry a pedigree. The contrast with an LLM that "is just sort of taking from everywhere and not citing anyone" (20:36). Useful for the point that formation has a lineage and a face while scraped text does not.
- 19:05 (Kalman): Maimonides quotes almost nobody in the Mishneh Torah as "an authority move" so the code speaks as "the voice of the tradition." Killip: that is what AI answers feel like, authoritative because unsourced (19:33).
- 21:50 (Tucker): in the academy plagiarism is the worst crime, but in the Beit Midrash "there is no greater joy than discovering that something you thought you made up is actually something the Ritva said."
- 27:02 (Kalman on Nahmanides, "do what is right and good"): "there are times when the law is telling you that something is totally fine, totally legal, but you're supposed to know that it's still the wrong thing to do." The cleanest statement in this batch that rules underdetermine goodness.
- 29:14 (Tucker): the Chatam Sofer's fisherman case and "ze neheneh ve-ze lo chaser" (the shade of another man's wall). A memorable image if Carl ever needs a Jewish-law example of benefiting without harming.
- 38:04 to 42:19 (Tucker): the Noda bi-Yehuda typesetting case, "a fast-moving resourceful printer and then a legal authority that is just trying to play catch-up" (Kalman, 43:37). Analogy to labs moving faster than the people who judge them.
- 51:30 (Kalman): if a rabbi ruled tomorrow that generative AI is theft, "it kind of creates a pole for people to respond to," versus the pattern where "the tech companies are the ones acting and everyone else is just reacting."
- 52:36 (Killip): "the speed is new, but the questions are ancient."
- Needs checking: the Esther citation (Megillah 15a is the usual source), the Noda bi-Yehuda case details, and Tucker's reference to a New York Times piece on Adobe terms of service with the "hardware store... doesn't get to own the painting" quote (42:20).

## Tension or objection

- The episode's method is precedent-based analogy, and Kalman admits responsa are "inherently biased towards the past" and do not allow that "nothing is relevant" (~03:30). A critic will say the same of Carl's talk: that reading alignment through religious practice assumes the analogy before testing it.
- Tucker will not give a bottom line; Kalman confesses "in my gut I want there to be a decision" (51:30). If Carl's practices list reads as frameworks rather than proposals, the same frustration applies.
- The episode treats the model as an economic artifact, scraped text redistributed, with no hint that anything is being formed; it is a reminder that a religiously literate audience may see LLMs as plagiarism machines first and minds second.
