# 02 — The Two Lamp Mystery, 2024-07-01

## Relevance

Medium. Not about AI, but the Amish and Orthodox Jewish cases are a clean field study of a community forming its members through costly, deliberately inefficient practice in service of an image of the good life, which is exactly the formation-versus-rules distinction the talk opens with.

## Who

- Host: DZ Kalman (Speaker 1; transcript renders "Davit Sviekallman"). He grew up Orthodox and studied religion and technology in graduate school.
- Guest: Lindsay Ems (Speaker 3), professor at Butler University, author of *Virtually Amish*, who did roughly eight years of fieldwork in Amish settlements in Indiana.
- Speakers 2, 4, 5 are clips and ads (a Jetsons clip, a Bose ad).

## Summary

Kalman opens with a childhood question: if the Jetsons are the future, how can Shabbat survive when electronics are in everything? He answers it with two lamps. An Amish wall lamp converted to run on a power-tool battery, and a kosher lamp whose inner sleeve blocks the light without turning it off. Both look like loophole engineering from outside; from inside, both show that the rule was never the point. The Amish refused the grid because it tied them to the outside world and made every further adoption frictionless; Jews refused electricity on Shabbat because it resembled work. Ems explains that the Ordnung, the Amish governing code, is oral, unwritten, and "communicated by lived behavior," that adoption decisions run through sermons and a slow democratic process that deliberately errs conservative, that the community's stated goal is salvation and its stated fear is independence from the community, and that smartphones, which cannot be kept out by physical distance, have pushed the Amish toward shoring up communal bonds rather than banning devices. Kalman closes: Shabbat was never about electronics but about an experience, and preserving it is an active negotiation, not a yes or no to each new tool.

## Bears on

- Thread 1 (formation versus rules): the whole episode. The Ordnung is not a text read into minds but a way of life transmitted by example (13:05). Both lamps show that a rule is a proxy for an image of the good (rest, dependence on community, closeness to God), and that outsiders who read the rule as the thing itself "have misunderstood the rule" (Kalman, ~05:00 to 07:00).
- Thread 4 (costly signaling): Ems's "speed bumps and guardrails." Kalman notes the deliberate inefficiency signals difference and marks the group as "inside the world and also outside of the world at the same time" (11:28). Ems: "efficiency is not necessarily the goal" when the goal is community well-being (13:05). The Amish "err on the side of conservatism" and the process is "lengthy, arduous, chaotic" by design.
- Thread 2 (mechanism of formation): the Amish response to smartphones is to form children before exposure, so that strong ties make the device "irrelevant" rather than forbidden (25:02 to 26:23). That is the talk's claim that a formed mind handles novel situations a rule could not anticipate.
- Thread 3 (hermeneutic of suspicion): Ems on control versus addiction, and on technology users being "used by the technology designers" (22:23). Mild relevance: a community that trusts its members' formation rather than policing them.

## Usable material

- 05:30 to 07:30 (Kalman monologue): the two lamps. A compact, visual parable: the Amish battery lamp and the kosher lamp both look like cheating; both make sense once you know what the rule is *for*. "From the outside it looks insincere. From the inside, it makes perfect sense." Good spoken-talk opener for formation versus rules.
- 13:05 (Ems): the Ordnung "is oral. It's not written down. And it's communicated to the people in that community by lived behavior." Direct support for "a text read into a mind is not the same as being formed by it."
- 13:05 (Ems): "efficiency is not necessarily the goal in most cases, if you're looking for community well-being." Pairs with costly signaling.
- 11:28 (Kalman on Shabbat elevators and speed bumps): the inefficiency "is useful in signaling to other people that this is different."
- 16:59 (Ems): what the community orients around. The goal is salvation; farming valued because the family works together and sees the connections between labor, food, rain, and sun. The fear is independence: "they felt dependence really tied people together into God." A one-line statement of an image of the good held in common.
- 19:54 (Kalman): the whole Amish system depends on unbaptized 16-year-olds who answer the business email because they are not yet bound by the rules. A funny, concrete example of how a formed community tolerates boundary cases a rulebook would forbid.
- 25:02 (Ems): on smartphones, an Amish informant said "we need to teach our children how to engage with one another before they're even born." Formation precedes the test; the community foregrounds "what makes people stronger as a result of being in this community" because the device will arrive regardless.
- 26:09 (Kalman): this "is kind of conceding that this is a technology that can't really be controlled and the only way to counteract it is to shore up... the strength and power of communal bonds." A direct analogue to alignment-by-formation when the environment cannot be sandboxed.
- 31:39 (Ems): "mutual respect for difference." Conservative Amish districts appreciated progressive neighbors and vice versa, the conservatives reminding the progressives "this is who we are." Contrast: the settlement that wanted uniform rules across 30 church groups had "a lot of trouble." Could support plural evals written by distinct traditions (thread 5) rather than one universal rulebook.
- 33:41 (Kalman close): Shabbat "isn't really about electronics. It's about an experience," and preserving it "has always been an active process, a kind of negotiation with a changing world that is so much more than looking at new tech and just saying yes or no." Rules are downstream of the image.
- Needs checking: Ems says the Amish population "is growing exponentially" and has "gone through the Industrial Revolution basically in the last 30 years" (29:57); Kalman says the Amish and Jews are "the only two places in American society where an entire people has decided to negotiate its own relationship with technology" (~07:30), which is rhetorical and contestable.

## Tension or objection

- Ems insists there is "not really a typical process" and that nothing learned about one Amish community generalizes to the next (13:05). If Carl presents religion as a lab with transferable results, this is the field-worker's objection: formation is local, slow, and non-generalizable, which is the opposite of what an ML training recipe needs.
- The Amish mechanism works by keeping the world out (physical distance, no grid) and it is visibly straining under smartphones, which Kalman calls "a battle that is inevitably going to be lost in some ways" (23:00). A frontier model cannot be kept from the world; the sandboxing half of the method does not transfer.
- The formation here rests on dependence and on a slow democratic process among people who share a life. A model being trained has neither a community of peers nor a vote. The analogy is to the child being raised, not to the adult deciding.
