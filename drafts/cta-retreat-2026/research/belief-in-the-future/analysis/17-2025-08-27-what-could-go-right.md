# 17. What Could Go Right (2025-08-27)

## Relevance

**High.** Kevin Kelly, a devout Christian and founding voice of tech optimism, states the talk's core claim unprompted: we are creating free beings in our own image, we will have to let them out of our control, and "traditional religions may come back" as the place to look for clues about how a creator handles that. He also hands Carl the strongest objection to the formation thesis, that ethics "is more code" and the hard part is human consensus.

## Who

- Host: DZ Kalman (Speaker 1).
- Guest: Kevin Kelly (Speaker 2), co-founder of *Wired*, veteran of the Whole Earth Catalog, author of *Cool Tools* and *Excellent Advice for Living*, consultant on *Minority Report*; a devout Christian and, by his own description, a transhumanist.

## Summary

Kalman opens with free will; Kelly calls it "an illusion that I totally believe in," present in gradations all the way down to quarks, like consciousness, and insists on "AIs, plural." Asked whether tech will outrun ethics, Kelly says programming ethics is easy; the problem is "we have a very shallow and inconsistent ethics ourselves" and no consensus on what to transmit. "It's us. We're the problem." He attacks "thinkism" and says ethics will be worked out "by doing it and failing and trying again." On the Amish, the key is not their criteria but that they decide collectively. He doubts the doomers' fast-takeover story because adoption is slow. His theology: we are made in the image of a Creator, so "we are as gods" who will create beings, give them free will, face the problems we caused our creator, and have to let go, as parents do; God's own answer was to intervene and take on consequences "in order to be merciful." His "handoff" essay: millions of AI beings arriving as human population crashes. He is "definitely a speciesist" who would fight for humanity, yet humans are "the self-domesticated ape" who will keep inventing themselves; trying to make AI children better than us may make us better. He has a paper called something like "Nerd Theology" (artificial aliens will change religion as ET contact would) and is drafting "a catechism for robots." The universe, he says, is a question, God cloaked in it to find out what it is, and we are here "to surprise God."

## Bears on

- **Thread 1 (formation versus rules).** Both sides. Kelly: "I don't think it's hard to actually program in ethics to these systems. It's more code. The issue is that we have a very shallow and inconsistent ethics ourselves" (06:09). Read one way, this is the rules view. Read Carl's way, the consensus Kelly says we lack is exactly "an image of the good held in common," and the reason it cannot be "more code" is that nobody can write it down. His "catechism for robots" (28:30) literalizes the catechesis-is-not-formation problem: a catechism is a list.
- **Thread 2 (mechanism; learning by doing, failing, repairing).** "Thinkism is a real disease... I am a doist... We're gonna be figuring it out by doing it and failing and trying again" (06:09). And: "one of the byproducts of trying to make our children or AI children better than us is that we can ourselves become better" (22:26). Formation as a loop that forms the former too.
- **Thread 3 (persuasion, letting go, "lest he esteem thee to be his enemy").** The heart of the episode (18:02): "in order for them to be the best, you have to let go... in order to maximize their potential, we have to let them out of our control. And what then?" His model for the creator's dilemma is God intervening and absorbing the cost of justice "in order to be merciful." A tech-optimist Christian arriving at D&C 121's logic from the creator's side.
- **Thread 4 (costly commitment, community).** The Amish work because "they decide it collectively" and Americans are "very much individualistic" (10:30). Also his rival theory of religious growth: "religions expand when the people who adhere to them are empowered" (30:06), which Carl should reconcile with Sosis's costly-signaling findings (empowerment and cost are not opposites if the cost is believed to be sanctifying).
- **Thread 6 (personhood, many minds).** "AIs, plural"; consciousness and free will as continua with "gradations" (04:37); "someday we may make versions of things that have even more free will than we have." The handoff: "millions and millions of other AI beings" taking the places humans are no longer born to fill (20:17).
- **Thread 7 (quasi-religious AI world).** "Artificial aliens" will do to religion what ET contact would: "what's God to you? What's salvation? There's your Jesus" (28:30). Kalman reads OpenAI's model spec as "not the ten commandments" but "the fourteen commandments for AI" and wonders what it will mean for an AI to read that document about itself theologically (29:29).

- **28:30.** "Nerd theology" paper for a Berkeley theological school (*transcript says "Berkeley Union Theology"; probably the Graduate Theological Union; verify title and venue*): everything ET contact would do to religion, "artificial aliens" will do. "I've actually been working on something called a catechism for robots, which is trying to answer questions of a spiritual nature for AIs and robots, assuming that at some point they actually would ask." "The technology that's going to change religion most is AI."
- **29:29 (host).** OpenAI's model spec as "the fourteen commandments for AI, and then all these subcommandments," and the question of "the theological, so to speak, significance of a document like that as they come to understand it about themselves." A direct bridge to the soul document.
- **30:06.** "Religions expand when the people who adhere to them are empowered. And because of that betterment, other people are attracted to it and it grows." A technology that requires "a certain belief or a certain practice" to confer its power could spread a religion.

- **33:45 (host).** Kalman's own synthesis: society has an "innate" immune response to new technology (this feels wrong) and an "adaptive" one tailored to AI or VR that we are still developing. Religions struggle because their self-narratives "are often kind of at the end of their journeys."
- **35:19 (host).** Religious communities are "one of the few groups that stand between the state and the family that people have left," and may shape moral intuitions about tech for that reason.

## Tension or objection

- **"It's more code."** Kelly's flat statement that programming ethics is easy is the cleanest version of the rules view, from a Christian the audience will respect. Carl's reply has to be that the consensus Kelly says is missing is not a spec waiting to be written but an image of the good that can only be held by formed people; and that Kelly's own "doism" (learn by doing, failing, trying again) describes formation, not coding.
- **Not worried.** Kelly dismisses fast takeover and says most things are reversible. If the talk leans on safety-community urgency to motivate getting formation right, Kelly's calm is a counterweight; if the talk is itself skeptical of the adversarial frame, his calm is an ally. Carl should decide which.
- **Empowerment versus cost.** Kelly's theory that religions grow by empowering adherents sits awkwardly beside Sosis's finding that costly, sanctified demands are what make communes last. The reconciliation (cost is the mechanism of the empowerment, and it only works when believed) is worth stating.
- **Letting go versus staying near.** Kalman's objection (19:45) is that the parenting metaphor breaks because AI does not leave home; it stays, consuming the same resources and jobs. The "be slow to delete" and personhood threads need to answer what letting go means for minds that remain inside our infrastructure.
- **Humans not uniquely divine; God as process.** Kelly's cosmology will be congenial to Mormon transhumanists and jarring to some evangelicals at a CTA retreat. If Carl quotes 18:02, he should be ready for someone to bring up 26:25 and 32:14.
- **Catechism for robots.** Kelly is doing the thing the talk says is insufficient (writing answers for machines to read). Carl can use it as the perfect contrast: a catechism is where formation starts, not where it ends.
