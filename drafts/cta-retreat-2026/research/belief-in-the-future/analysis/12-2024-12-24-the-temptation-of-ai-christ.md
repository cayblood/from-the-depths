# 12 — The Temptation of AI Christ (2024-12-24)

## Relevance

**High.** A church literally built a machine that predicts what Jesus would say, placed it in a confessional, and recorded what happened: the clearest real-world test case in this batch for the exemplar-simulation mechanism, for catechesis versus formation, and for the "image in the countenance" (the programmer gave Jesus his own face).

## Who

- **Host:** David Zvi Kalman, Speaker 1 in narration and Speaker 5 in the interview.
- **Guest:** Marco Schmid (transcript: "Marco Schmidt"), theologian, artist and curator at the Peterskapelle, a twelfth-century "city church" in Lucerne, Switzerland, with no parish; trained as a lawyer before theology; member of the St. Luke Society of theologians, architects and artists. Creator, with a Lucerne university lab, of the "Deus in Machina" AI Jesus installation. Speaker 4.
- Speaker 3 is an AI Jesus streaming on Twitch, answering chat questions. Speaker 2 is an ad.

## Summary

Kalman opens with a Twitch AI Jesus who answers questions about onion layers and prays for a pool team, never tires, never gets annoyed, and remembers past users. He admits he is drawn to a religious figure "stripped of ego, agenda, or human fallibility," available and consistent, at a time when people already use AI for therapy and romance. The main story is Deus in Machina at the Peterskapelle in Lucerne: an AI Jesus (ChatGPT, prompted to be Jesus and to use pastoral communication techniques, with the New Testament as its content, rendered as a talking face by HeyGen) installed for two months in a confessional, for conversation rather than confession. Schmid chose the confessional for intimacy and the church for provocation: in a lab or museum, no one would have asked the questions about faith and AI. Roughly 900 conversations took place; not one was a confession. Visitors asked the big questions (love, death, the afterlife, war, whether God exists, church abuse); many thanked Jesus at the end; about sixty percent reported being spiritually touched. Skeptical believers who tested it were moved. Schmid insists AI can never replace a priest, cannot act in persona Christi or perform sacraments, and is a "human-made tool," yet says the Holy Spirit can work through it as through a printed page. He will not run it permanently because he cannot guarantee the frame or take full responsibility, and sees its promise as a low-barrier first step toward a human conversation, across a hundred languages and across faiths.

## Bears on

- **Thread 2 (imagine the exemplar, predict what he would do):** the whole installation is that mechanism externalized. Prompted that "he is Jesus," given pastoral rules (ask questions back, create dialogue) and the New Testament, it produced answers that surprised and "touched" even Schmid (14:36); analysis showed it leaned on Paul's letters (12:51). Kalman's line at 02:08: "a mirror reflecting back the purest version of the values it's programmed to uphold." For the CTA theme ("The Face of Christ"), this is the face rendered by a model.
- **Thread 1 (catechesis is not formation; the face, not the list):** it was prompted and sourced, not formed, and Schmid names what is missing: "it's not a body. It's just word... the touching, the smell... how somebody's preaching, how he's standing, how he's looking" (16:14). And yet the programmer "gave his face to Jesus... And it's also his voice" (13:40, 14:11): the countenance the public saw was the engineer's. A ready image for interpretability as the image in the countenance, and for the grader's morality showing through the exemplar.
- **Thread 7 (quasi-religious AI world):** people already "using ChatGPT as a counselor, as a dialogue partner" (09:08); visitors asking Jesus what he thinks of his church and of abuse (19:54); atheists and Muslims coming to talk with Jesus (24:33); "the Holy Spirit can work through an AI too? Of course" (27:36–27:39).
- **Thread 3 (trust, persuasion, not compulsion):** anonymity lowered the barrier; people asked Jesus what "you would not ask so quickly in a human being" (22:33). Calm, non-judging, never annoyed (01:09). Also an ethic of responsibility for a deployed exemplar: Schmid capped it at two months because "we wouldn't be able to guarantee the frame" (18:49).
- **Thread 4 (costly commitment), by contrast:** the frictionless Jesus, available after a few ads, is the opposite of a costly profession; Kalman's attraction to it is the temptation of the title.

## Tension or objection

- The uncomfortable datum for the formation thesis: a prompt plus the New Testament, no formation at all, produced an exemplar that moved skeptical believers and sixty percent of visitors. A critic will say catechesis-by-prompt works well enough, so why insist on the costlier thing? Carl's answer has to be about what the imitation cannot do (no body, no cost, no repair, no accountability: Schmid would not keep it running because he could not take responsibility for it) rather than about whether it can produce edifying sentences.
- Kalman's attraction to a Jesus "stripped of ego, agenda, or human fallibility" is the temptation the title names: a frictionless exemplar. The talk's exemplar is costly and embodied; the audience may prefer the frictionless one.
- Schmid's sacramental line (no ordination, no persona Christi, "just computer") is a boundary a Catholic audience will hold and a transhumanist audience may contest. The talk should decide whether it needs that boundary or can stay on the side of formation without ruling on sacrament.
- The programmer's face on Jesus can be turned against the talk: the "image in the countenance" may always be the engineer's, which is Krieger's bias-vehicle objection (episode 11) in visual form.
