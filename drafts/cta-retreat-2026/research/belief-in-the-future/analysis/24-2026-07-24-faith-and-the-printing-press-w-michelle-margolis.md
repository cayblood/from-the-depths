# 24. Faith and the printing press, with Michelle Margolis (2026-07-24)

## Relevance

Low. A history episode with no alignment content, but it offers a few images for the costly-signaling thread: a sacred scroll must be handwritten with intention (kavanah) even though printing was cheaper, and a scribe's year of labor was itself a signal of a book's trustworthiness that print erased and approbations had to replace. Otherwise its value is as analogy for the talk's framing of technological transitions.

## Who

- Host: DZ Kalman (Speaker 1), who describes himself as a historian.
- Guest: Michelle Margolis (Speaker 2), Norman E. Alexander Librarian for Jewish Studies at Columbia University and past president of the Association of Jewish Libraries. Identified by the host.

## Summary

Kalman argues that history's contribution to the AI conversation is not technology but people and how they respond when their world changes, and that print was one of the first revolutions people saw coming in real time. Margolis explains the material basis: rag paper (not trees), movable type matrices that fit in a shoebox, a press that was a wooden screw anyone could build, and a workshop that mixed scholars, strong men, women and children with small fingers, Jews and Christians, in a way a scriptorium never did. Early Hebrew printers called the press miraculous. Luther was "an early modern influencer" issuing pamphlets; the Church took about seventy-five years to regulate, then burned the Talmud and built the Index. Because anyone could print, the old signals of trust collapsed and the Jewish world developed the haskamah (approbation). Rabbis complained that "many ignorant people stubbornly print their books," that printers altered texts, and that codes would stop people consulting rabbis. Print contracted liturgical diversity, enabled glossed texts like the Rema on the Shulchan Aruch, and was refused for sacred scrolls on grounds of intentionality. Margolis ends by warning that abundant information you can look up is information you never hold in your head.

## Bears on

- Thread 4 (costly signaling): at 18:02 Kalman observes that a scribe's year of labor on a book told you, before you opened it, that the book was trustworthy; print removed that signal, so new mechanisms (approbations, censorship) had to replace it. At 27:20 to 29:38, sacred scrolls stayed handwritten because writing one "is a sacred act" requiring kavanah and ritual immersion at each divine name; the cost was the point.
- Thread 1 (formation versus rules), indirectly: at 34:09 Margolis argues that people who merely look things up do not make the connections that people who have read and internalized them make; the scientific discoverers "actually sat down and read those books." Having information available is not having been formed by it.
- Thread 7, as analogy only: "the printers think it's amazing, kinda like how when you hear from somebody from Anthropic talking about AI" (21:26); the people in power are the ones who worry.
- Thread 3, faintly: the Church trusted Christians to excise heretical pages themselves but not Jews, so Jewish books had to be submitted to inquisitors who did not know Hebrew and crossed out the wrong words (12:00). A picture of suspicion producing clumsy, ignorant enforcement.

## Usable material

- 03:41: A printed book cost about a day laborer's annual salary, "like buying a car"; Mantua censor records show every Jewish household had one to five books. Peter the Venerable in 1141 sneering at paper "made from the scrap of old rags or perhaps from even viler stuff." (Check the quotation.)
- 05:03: Paper was cloth, not trees, until the nineteenth century; crumbling books are the new ones. Type matrices fit in a shoebox; a scribe took a year per codex, a printer a year per edition of hundreds or thousands.
- 08:29: The print shop as a meeting of classes and religions: editors, "a very strong person who's the person who's pulling the press," women and children who could match letters without reading.
- 12:00: Early Hebrew printers called the press "miraculous," amazed at a commentary on the same page as the Bible. Luther "like a modern TikToker ... putting out reels like nobody's business," including the trajectory from hoping Jews would convert to *On the Jews and Their Lies*. The Church took seventy-five years to regulate; the Talmud burned in Rome in the 1550s and appeared in every edition of the Index. Censors crossed out "goy" even where it meant the Jewish nation, and hair-cutting laws because the root resembled the word for a tonsured priest.
- 18:02: Kalman: "the very fact of a book's existence says a lot about its authority"; a scribe's time was a signal. Print erased it, so "you need to create all these new mechanisms both for blocking ... and for positively signaling." Margolis: the haskamah. Directly usable for the costly-signal thread.
- 18:49: Seventeenth-century Rabbi Jonah Landsofer: in manuscript days "only people with something worthwhile to say would write books," but now "many ignorant people stubbornly print their books in a desire to become famous." (Check attribution and wording.) Also the rabbi who warned against printed books because printers "will make things up," whose text survives only because it was printed.
- 21:26: "The printers think it's amazing, kinda like how when you hear from somebody from Anthropic talking about AI." The Rema choosing to print his Ashkenazi law as a gloss on Karo's Sephardic Shulchan Aruch, "subservient to his text," two scholars in conversation on one page; and Karo banning a printed romance from Shabbat reading.
- 25:10: Print contracted diversity: prayer books claimed to cover Germany, Poland, and Lithuania at once; the Comtat Venaissin communities kept copying their liturgy by hand into the eighteenth century because no printer would invest. A caution about homogenization through scale.
- 27:20: A printed Esther scroll in the sixteenth century: the rabbi ordered all copies destroyed lest someone mistake it for a handwritten scroll. "Whether sacred books can be printed, and the answer is emphatically no." Intentionality (kavanah) is the reason; a scribe immerses at each writing of the divine name. Margolis contrasts machine matzah, which was accepted. (Check the Esther-scroll responsum.)
- 32:06: When Maimonides wrote his code, the backlash was "if you write all the laws, then nobody's gonna go ask a rabbi." "We literally have the same questions today. If you have rabbi AI, then nobody's gonna ask a rabbi." Kalman: "the history of rabbis being concerned that no one's gonna come talk to them." Benjamin Slonik's guide for women because "oral knowledge is not good enough anymore in the age of print."
- 33:29: A Jewish printer printing anti-Jewish polemic "because he's a businessman."
- 34:09: Margolis's soapbox: with so much information "you don't read it ... you no longer have it in your head. You just look it up"; "when you actually learn it, you then can make connections that you don't make when you're just like, okay, I'm just gonna check what the answer is." The discoverers were those who read and talked in salons.

## Tension or objection

- The episode's deepest lesson is that print's winners were often those who made the content cheaper and more uniform, and that the old costly signals were replaced by institutional gatekeeping (approbations, the Index) rather than preserved. A skeptic could say that costly signaling does not survive technological transitions; it gets substituted by bureaucracy. Carl's thread 4 should say why formation costs cannot simply be replaced by certification.
- Margolis's intentionality point cuts both ways: the sacred scroll stayed handwritten, but matzah went to the machine. The tradition drew the line by what was sacred, not by what was costly; if Carl says cost itself does the work, this is a counterexample.
- The Church's suspicion of Jewish books produced ignorant, over-broad censorship (crossing out "goy"), which supports the hermeneutic-of-suspicion thread, but the Church's trust of Christians to self-censor was not obviously better either; trust and suspicion were allocated by in-group, not by evidence.
