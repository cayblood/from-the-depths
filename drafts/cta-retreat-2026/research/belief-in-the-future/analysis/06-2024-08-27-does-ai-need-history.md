# 06 — Does AI Need History? (released as "AI's First 3,000 Years"), 2024-08-27

## Relevance

Medium. A historian of medieval automata supplies the disposable-creatures material for thread 6 (Star Trek's "army of Datas," Aristotle, slavery), a praying-monk automaton that models formation as embodied practice for threads 1 and 2, and a structural objection, that artificial servants have always served elites and "worsen the treatment of those whose humanity is already questioned," that Carl should be ready for.

## Who

- Host: DZ Kalman (Speaker 1; transcript renders "David Svikalman"), who came to AI through the history of technology and medieval Jewish and Islamic law.
- Guest: Ellie Truett (Speaker 3; spelling as rendered by the transcript), historian of technology at the University of Pennsylvania and author of *Medieval Robots*.
- Speaker 2 is an archival clip of a Dartmouth 1956 organizer describing the summer study (not named in the transcript). Speaker 7 is a Star Trek: The Next Generation clip from "The Measure of a Man." Speakers 4 through 6 and 8 are ads.

## Summary

Kalman argues that the 1956 Dartmouth workshop is the wrong origin story for AI. Borrowing Stanisław Lem, he says Dartmouth was the moment method started to matter, when computers made the old dream plausible, but the dream of making "something that reminds us of ourselves" is thousands of years old, in Hephaestus's servants, the Golem, and real automata. Truett describes Hellenistic mechanical tableaux, a statue of Isis dispensing milk for Cleopatra, and the tenth-century Throne of Solomon in Constantinople with roaring lions and singing birds, which an Italian envoy could only half explain while Greek sources treated it as scripted ritual. Latin Christendom, having lost the mechanical knowledge, read such objects as magic and demons. The literary automata, she argues, encode elite anxieties: virgin-detecting lutes, guards that bar the non-noble, Virgil's Salvatio Romae pointing toward rebellious provinces, prophetic brass heads that answer questions of power. She would start the history of AI "with a history of human beings," as "another iteration of trying to get something for nothing." A sixteenth-century praying Dominican friar automaton taught correct devotional posture to induce the right interior state. On Data's trial, she says these objects raise "what is our ethical obligation to what we create," and that if AI still serves the powerful it "cannot possibly be transformative." Kalman closes: higher stakes do not make anyone grow up.

## Bears on

- Thread 6 (personhood, copies, deletion): the central hit. The "Measure of a Man" clip (39:39): "an army of Datas, all disposable... Whole generations of disposable people. He's talking about slavery." Kalman: you "can make a million Datas and they act like people in every respect, and yet you've afforded yourself the right to not treat them like people" (39:22). Truett: these objects emerge where "enslaved people are everywhere" and Aristotle calls women natural slaves (37:46), and they worsen treatment of those "whose humanity is already questioned."
- Threads 1 and 2 (formation as embodied practice): the Dominican friar automaton (34:19 to 36:32) "intended to model the correct way" to pray so that "by doing that with your body, you would induce your mind to be open to ecstatic divinity," and "it is teaching a particular affect... the production of sentiment in a person. And it's interesting to see that come from a machine." A machine as exemplar for human formation; the reversal of Carl's direction, and a good hinge.
- Thread 3 (hermeneutic of suspicion): indirect. The Salvatio Romae (27:46), a statue that points at the rebelling province, is surveillance as the first imagined use of an artificial mind; the prophetic heads whose users are "so dumb, can't interpret it correctly" (29:22) is the oracle-you-cannot-trust motif.
- Thread 7 (quasi-religious AI): the magic-versus-mechanism history (17:15 to 20:46) shows that the same object is read as sorcery or engineering depending on what the reader knows how to build; Kalman's Lem argument is a general account of why AI talk goes religious when method is uncertain.
- Thread 4 (costly signaling): nothing direct.

## Usable material

- 01:50 to 06:30 (Kalman, Lem's cancer analogy): "In the world that can't cure cancer, magic and medicine are basically the same." Dartmouth is not where the dream began but where "we first looked at computers and said... I think these things could make those dreams real." Good for explaining why AI discourse is full of religious language: the dream predates the method.
- 11:55 (Truett): students must grapple with "how much technology has been naturalized" (clothes, eyeglasses, agriculture, the alphabet) and that people long ago "do not have less sophisticated minds. They may have less sophisticated tools." Useful if Carl needs to defend taking ancient practice seriously as technology.
- 13:55 (Truett): "What separates history from prehistory? Writing. That is a technology." Ages are named for their tools.
- 14:47 (Truett): societies adopt technologies when they need them (the Inca had no use for the wheel), but now "state or corporate actors... push us," which is the inevitability theme from the trailer stated by a historian.
- 16:32 (Truett): Cleopatra's Isis statue that stood, sat, and dispensed milk; a machine built to present a ruler as a goddess.
- 20:55 to 23:39 (Truett): the Throne of Solomon. The Italian envoy "made sure I knew what to expect" so as not to lose composure; the Greek source records only when the lions roar and when the human servant speaks. "The emperor is making a statement that he has human servants and artificial servants at his command and they speak for him. Like he never speaks." Also Kalman's Esther-midrash memory of the throne that mauls the Persian king (23:39). Vivid and short enough for a spoken talk.
- 24:35 (Kalman on Homer, citing Martin Devecka): whether Hephaestus's automata get built is "a choice," and they were not built because "slaves are so much cheaper." "Will plays a much, much larger role than we often understand."
- 25:50 (Truett): the lute that breaks if a non-virgin enters and the guard that bars the non-noble: fantasies of "perfectly predictable, docile, unerring servants" born of "elites being concerned about real elite stuff."
- 29:22 (Truett): the prophetic brass heads attributed to Albertus Magnus, Roger Bacon, and Pope Sylvester II; they are asked "will I have power?" never "was I my mother's favorite." Kalman's quip: "not, can you tell me how to fold a given protein" (31:17).
- 33:16 (Truett): AI as "another iteration of trying to get something for nothing... like a perpetual motion machine," the desire for something that "can serve human needs and desires better than humans can without the messiness of being human."
- 34:19 to 36:32 (Truett): the Smithsonian's Dominican friar automaton, made for the emperor, that walks and gestures in the sequence the order prescribed; "it is teaching a particular affect." The best single image in the episode for Carl: a machine built to form a human by showing him what the practice looks like.
- 37:03 to 37:46: Kalman, people "attribute some kind of humanity to objects that look human"; Truett, "isn't it such a paradox that people are also, it seems, reluctant to extend that view of humanity to human beings?"
- 37:46 (Truett): "These objects don't help us understand humanity as a larger, more capacious group... it worsens the treatment of those individuals whose humanity is already questioned." And: "what do we really want from these objects? And are we prepared to deal with the consequences of getting what we want?"
- 39:22 to 40:51: the Data trial. Kalman: "that was the point of making Data in the first place... all the benefits of putting a human being to work and none of the obligations whatsoever."
- 40:51 (Truett): "what is our ethical obligation to what we create?" and the LLM point: trained on human output "that is not getting paid for. There is something really exploitative ultimately about these."
- 42:32 (Kalman's close): "giving a person more responsibility does not automatically make that person grow up"; AI "has always been a place for people to play out the idea of getting service without a cost," and "the fact that today's AI is real is no guarantee that we're suddenly going to grow up."
- Needs checking: the Throne of Solomon account (the Italian envoy is presumably Liudprand of Cremona, not named in the transcript); the claim that a Byzantine envoy saw a similar device at the Abbasid palace in Samarra and installed one twenty-five years later as emperor (23:15); the Smithsonian friar automaton's provenance and the "made for the emperor" claim; the "Martin De Vecca" article on Homeric automata (spelling uncertain); and Kalman's claim that the Data episode "influenced an entire generation of AI researchers" (40:29).

## Tension or objection

- Truett's structural claim (40:51): artificial servants have always served the interests of the powerful, so "if we're still seeing them serving the interests of the people in power, then they cannot possibly be transformative or revolutionary." A talk that proposes better formation of models inside labs owned by the powerful will hear this as rearranging the furniture. Carl should say who the formation serves.
- Her reading that humanoid objects "worsen the treatment of those whose humanity is already questioned" (37:46) is a direct caution against thread 6: extending personhood talk to models has historically cut the other way, cheapening the humans at the margin. Kalman's "ickiness" (37:29) is the same instinct.
- The elite-anxiety reading (virgin detectors, non-noble barriers, rebellion alarms) implies that what we build into a servant encodes our fears, which is the hermeneutic-of-suspicion point made about the builders rather than the built: the adversarial frame may be the oldest frame.
- Kalman's closing, that higher stakes do not produce maturity, is a quiet rebuke to any claim that the current moment will finally make people take formation seriously.
