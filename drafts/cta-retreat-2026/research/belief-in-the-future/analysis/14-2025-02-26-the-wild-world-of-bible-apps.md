# 14. The Wild World of Bible Apps (2025-02-26)

## Relevance

**Medium.** The history of Bible software is beside the point, but Dyer's thesis that "technology is formative" and his finding that the same text read on different media produces different pictures of God give Carl an empirical illustration that getting a text into a mind is not the same as being formed by it.

## Who

- Host: DZ Kalman (Speaker 1).
- Guest: John Dyer (Speaker 2), professor at Dallas Theological Seminary (half teaching theology and sociology, half administration), former software developer and youth pastor, author of *People of the Screen*, a study of how evangelicals have shaped Bible software over forty years.

## Summary

Kalman opens with the claim that religious communities, usually laggards with technology, have always been early adopters of information technology (the codex, KDKA radio, Chabad.org among the first 500 domains), and with McLuhan's warning that making a text more accessible always changes the text. Dyer walks through four eras of digital Bible (academic indexing, desktop software for clergy, the web and Bible Gateway, mobile apps led by YouVersion) and argues that evangelical "hopeful entrepreneurial pragmatism" drove each one: "if it works, we'll do it." The predicted remix of the canon never happened; the battleground moved to the meta-content, the devotional plans and study notes that frame the text. His own study found print readers of Jude saw God's judgment and felt encouraged, while phone readers saw God's faithfulness and felt discouraged. People now leave the Bible playing as ambient audio to reinforce identity rather than to study. AI chatbots meant for theology questions are being used as confessors and counselors. Dyer closes with a theology of technology as a good but formative gift, "like fire," to be used with awe and put in its place, because we read scripture "for an encounter with God," and we are "called to be developed to be more like Jesus."

## Bears on

- **Thread 1 (formation versus rules).** The strongest connection. Dyer's data: the medium forms the reader's image of God independently of the text's content (29:19). He also says the real competition among apps is over the framing material around the canon, not the canon itself (17:53), which is a precise analogue to the claim that whatever surrounds the text (the grader, the reward) is what does the teaching. And: "we're in the most biblically illiterate time we ever have been in" despite the highest access (34:18). Access to text is not catechesis, and catechesis is not formation.
- **Thread 2 (mechanism of formation).** "I don't think we're reading just for information. I think we're reading for an encounter with God and with other people" (43:25). His tradition's telos is "to develop to be more like Jesus," with technology either formative or deformative toward that. The gym analogy (tools shape the body part they target) is a usable image for what training data does to a model.
- **Thread 7 (AI as spiritual counselor).** BibleChat.ai was built for theology questions and became a confessor; AI answers are "clean and crisp" but refuse to leave tension; the best chaplain sometimes "doesn't answer but just kind of sits with you" (39:42).
- **Thread 4 (costly signaling), indirectly.** "The easier we make it, the less people are willing to put in effort" (34:18). And people who choose to attend church online turn out more committed than some who attend in person, because choosing is itself a signal (35:45).
- **Thread 2 again: ambient immersion.** People leave the Bible playing all day "to reinforce who they believe they are" rather than to reach conclusions (23:56). Formation by saturation rather than by study; a possible analogue for narrative training data or context distillation.

## Usable material

- **00:04 (host).** Information-technology early adopters: Christians took up the codex, KDKA broadcast church services weeks after going on air in 1920, a televangelist won a 1953 Emmy over Lucille Ball, Chabad.org was among the first 500 domains. *All host claims; spot-check before using.* Also the YouVersion subway ad in which Satan gives the app zero stars.
- **14:46.** "The old joke that every new technology, the first thing on it is pornography. Well, the second thing is Bible."
- **16:16.** "Hopeful entrepreneurial pragmatism." Developers asked about design choices "would always just say, well, if it works, we'll do it." A fair description of RL culture too.
- **17:53.** Sociologists expected the Bible to "lose its covers" in the digital age; it didn't. "The battleground is not on the text itself but on the sort of metatext." Study-Bible notes have always pushed readers in particular directions (Geneva Bible, Scofield).
- **23:56.** Ambient Bible audio while cooking or mowing: "trying to reinforce who they believe they are, more than trying to come to a particular conclusion."
- **29:19.** The Jude study, a couple hundred readers at several churches: "more of the print readers said they thought it was about God's judgment, whereas more of the phone readers said that they thought it was about God's faithfulness." Print readers felt encouraged; phone readers felt discouraged and confused. Dyer's explanation (phones carry our anxieties; social media shares only the cheerful verses) is, in his words, "my guess." *Small sample; cite as suggestive.*
- **32:16.** Print is not neutral either: for thousands of years scripture was experienced "auditorially in a community of faith," and verse numbers are themselves a technology that shapes what people see. Good inoculation against a listener who thinks the talk is nostalgic for books.
- **34:18.** "We're in the most biblically illiterate time we ever have been in and yet we have the highest access level." "The easier we make it, the less people are willing to put in effort."
- **37:21.** BibleChat.ai: people started with "what is forgiveness" and quickly moved to "how do you forgive your husband when he sleeps with somebody else." Users treat a non-judgmental AI as a priest.
- **39:42.** AI answers "feel somewhat generic... very clean and crisp. But yet it feels like it doesn't get down very far." "Sometimes the best chaplain is that one that doesn't answer but just kind of sits with you." "The AI doesn't seem to want to leave a lot of tension."
- **40:46.** Dyer as a young youth pastor bought a Compaq iPaq with a Bible on it so he would never forget anything when a kid asked a question: "I thought, this will make me feel secure. But then I ended up feeling this really weird dependence on it."
- **43:25.** "I always see technology as formative, sometimes in surprising and unexpected ways. So just like we go into a gym and we choose tools based on which body part it has highlighted." "I don't think that we're called to be static. I think we're called to be developed... to be more like Jesus." Technology "is a good gift from God, but it's also a very powerful one, like fire."

## Tension or objection

- **Embodiment is not a trump card.** Dyer's post-pandemic data (35:45) found that people who attend church online are more committed and read scripture more than some in-person attenders. If the talk leans hard on "a face, not a list" and on embodied presence, someone will ask whether formation is really about bodies or about chosen, costly engagement in any medium.
- **Formation by media is not formation by exemplar.** The Jude finding shows media shaping readers, but it shows shaping by ambient mood and format, not by a person. Carl's thesis is more specific than Dyer's, and he should not let the data carry more than it can.
- **A tradition can form badly.** Dyer admits social media selects for the pleasant verses and that study notes have always pushed sectarian agendas. If a tradition's formation apparatus (its meta-content) can distort the text, then "let the traditions form the models" needs a reply about which tradition, which notes, and who checks.
- **The AI that avoids tension is a formed AI.** Dyer's "clean and crisp" chatbot is what current post-training produces. If Carl claims formation on an exemplar would fix this, he should be able to say what a model that "sits with you and doesn't answer" would look like in training terms.
