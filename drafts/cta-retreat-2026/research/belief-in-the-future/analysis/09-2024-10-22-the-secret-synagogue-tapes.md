# 09 — The Secret Synagogue Tapes (2024-10-22)

## Relevance

**Medium.** No AI content, but the most concrete picture in this batch of formation by exemplar: cantorial students learned the craft from bootleg recordings of masters leading real services, because the polished studio piece could not teach what the master did "on the spot." It also contains a sharp aside on how being observed changes performance, and a story of rule-breaking tolerated for a deeper good.

## Who

- **Host:** David Zvi Kalman (transcript: "David Speakalman"), Speaker 1; also Speaker 6 in the Berkson interview.
- **Jeremiah Lockwood**, musician and scholar of cantorial music, Speaker 4 (16:11).
- **Tom Berkson** (transcript gives both "Bergson" and "Berkson"), retired cantor living in Hollywood, California; entered the Jewish Theological Seminary at 27 with no high school diploma and organized friends to record cantors secretly in the late 1960s. Speaker 7.
- **Pamela Feld**, daughter of Mel Feld, Speaker 8.
- **Mel Feld** (d. 2015, age 87), Holocaust survivor and prolific synagogue recorder, heard in archival video testimony, Speaker 2.
- **Dudu Fisher**, cantor, quoted and briefly heard, Speaker 5.
- Speaker 3 reads translations of the 1948 Ben Stonehill recordings.

## Summary

Kalman tells the story of "live davenings," bootleg recordings of synagogue services made in violation of Sabbath law and social norms, and asks why so many people independently decided that was acceptable. He begins with Mel Feld at fifteen, standing in his family's emptied house in Poland in 1942, choosing what to carry: prayer books, tefillin, his father's tallit. After the Holocaust the urge to preserve became "almost holy": Ben Stonehill recorded a thousand displaced survivors singing in a Manhattan hotel lobby in 1948; Robert Lachmann had recorded the Jews of Djerba in 1929 as a scholar. From the 1950s, cantors and cantorial students taped services in Catskills resorts and New York synagogues, since studio records gave no realistic sense of how the masters prayed. Berkson sent friends in with recorders under their coats and says nobody was ever caught. Feld recorded for forty years, later with a spy watch, and his tapes are now an underground archive used by students. His daughter is torn: the value is obvious and it "wasn't exactly the right thing to do." Kalman concludes the recorders saw performances as windows into a heritage that belonged to everyone and was meant to be built upon, and ends with the image of sand through fingers: you cannot catch most of it, but what sticks is beautiful.

## Bears on

- **Thread 2 (mechanism of formation):** Berkson wanted live davenings to study "their styling, their phrasing, their improvisation" (21:34), and explains why the studio set piece fails: "You're getting him on the spot. You don't know what their temperament is on that shabbat... how much they have to give... what kind of creativity they'll come and improvise" (25:26). Formation needs the exemplar in a concrete, unrepeatable situation, not a polished output. Lockwood: live davenings are "the entirety of a service," not "short pieces extracted" (16:11). That is an argument for narrative, whole-situation training data over curated snippets.
- **Thread 3 (adversarial frame, honeypots):** Kalman's distinction between studio and field recording: "studio performers are acting for the microphone, while field recorders usually want their subjects to act like they're not being recorded at all. The easiest way to do this is to just not tell anybody that you're recording" (in the 03:37 block, roughly 08:00). This is the logic of covert evaluation stated plainly, together with its costs: it breaks law and trust, and the recorders knew it.
- **Thread 4 (costly signaling, rules versus the good they serve):** the recorders broke Sabbath law for posterity; Dudu Fisher, asked whether he minded being taped on the Sabbath: "I'm not the policeman of God" (29:16). A tradition with strict rules tolerating breach in service of a deeper good.
- **Thread 6 (deletion, preservation):** Feld choosing what to save from the darkened house (03:28–03:37); Stonehill's tapes as "the audio equivalent of a library that has been burned to the ground" (14:38); the preservationist's creed that the world "deserves to be captured" (31:48). All of this sits beside "be slow to delete."
- **Thread 1 (formation produces something one's own):** Pamela on her father, who did not have the best voice: "he replicated a lot of what he studied and learned... everything was done out of just a pure love" (30:10). Imitation of exemplars became his own voice, which moved a room to tears.

## Tension or objection

- The episode endorses individuals who overrode the tradition's explicit rules on their own judgment of a higher good, and no one was harmed. If formation means a mind that knows when to break the rule, the obvious question is who decides and how you would know in advance. Carl should have an answer about disclosed trials and accountability (Pamela's ambivalence is the honest version).
- The covert-observation point cuts both ways. The talk criticizes honeypots and lying to models, yet here secret observation was the only way to capture authentic behavior, and the observed performers, once told, mostly shrugged. An AI-safety listener will say the same of evals: you cannot learn what the model really does if it knows it is on the record. Carl needs the distinction between a community that would plausibly consent (Fisher) and a subject deceived in order to be punished.
