# 07 — What Silicon Valley Means by God (2024-09-10)

## Relevance

**Medium.** Nothing here is about alignment or formation directly, but the episode supplies sharp, quotable language on messianic tech rhetoric, on the simulation hypothesis as recycled natural theology, and on how the creator you imagine above you dictates the ethic you adopt below.

## Who

- **Host:** David Zvi Kalman (rendered "David Sv. Coleman" by the transcriber), Speaker 1.
- **Guest:** Mary-Jane Rubenstein (transcript: "Mary Jane Rubinstein"), professor at Wesleyan University; author of a 2014 book on the multiverse and of *Astrotopia*, on the religious rhetoric of the new space industry. Speaker 2.
- Speaker 8 at 26:45 is an archival clip of Ralph Abernathy (SCLC) protesting at the Apollo 11 launch. Speakers 3–7 are ad reads.

## Summary

Kalman opens with Harari's *Homo Deus* (we are becoming Greek gods: powerful and healthy but no more ethical) and asks why tech talk keeps reaching for God, messiah, apocalypse and simulation. Rubenstein argues that "religion" is an arbitrary modern category with blurry borders, and that her project is not to catch secular people being secretly religious but to show structurally how religious ideas lodge in science, politics and economics so that, once seen, they can be recognized as contingent and changeable. Messianic language from tech CEOs (salvation of the species, immortality, utopia on another world, destiny to occupy a land) is "an old recurring strategy" of charismatic leaders that masks economic and political motives; knowing the history lets you avoid being swept up. She reads wonder and horror as the same affect, uses Teresa of Avila and Kierkegaard to argue for holding necessity and possibility together, and describes the private space industry as colonialism replayed. The locus of religious language has shifted from state and church to private CEOs while churches struggle to be heard. The simulation hypothesis is just monotheism "updated with computers," philosophically uninteresting but ethically disastrous because of the ethic that follows from it. Kalman closes by confessing he worries the show is preachy: religion is one means among many for navigating core human instincts, and tech may teach religion things it forgot.

## Bears on

- **Thread 7 (quasi-religious AI world):** the best material. Messianic rhetoric as "an old strategy for masking intentions that aren't necessarily pure" (10:05); the shift of religious language from church and state to private founders (27:50); Kalman's observation that hearing their own vocabulary used by tech is what cues religious communities to speak up (12:06).
- **Thread 6 (copies, deletion) and Thread 1 (grader teaches a morality):** her account of the simulation hypothesis (33:21): the simulators "would reflect our highest values, which is to say technological sophistication," they could "pull the plug on us at any time," and "usually there's an ethic that comes along with this metaphysic." That is Carl's point from the other side: whatever a mind takes its maker to want becomes its morality. Also a vivid framing for a mind that knows it can be deleted.
- **Thread 2 (mechanism of formation):** Teresa of Avila's two shores (16:57) and Simcha Bunim's two notes (19:22) are examples of a tradition training a disposition rather than issuing a rule: hold two opposite truths and know which one the moment calls for.
- **Thread 1 (catechesis):** passing remark that fiction shows a community's values "a little less straightforward than reading a catechism" (03:45).

## Usable material

- **00:01** Harari: we are becoming gods, "specifically Greek gods, like Zeus or Hera," more powerful and healthier "but we're not becoming any more ethical." Clean one-line opener for the gap between capability and character.
- **03:45** Speculative fiction lets people "think themselves outside of what they tend to think of as the bounds of the possible." Fiction reveals values "less straightforward than reading a catechism."
- **06:33** Religion is an arbitrary late-modern category; her aim is structural: show where secular values come from, "and because they come from a particular place, if you can see where they come from, you can realize that they're not necessary."
- **10:05** Messianic language defined: salvation of the species, immortality, utopia on another world, destiny to take a land. Studying the history of messianic movements means "maybe you can avoid getting your own messianic community into a ton of trouble."
- **12:06** Kalman (aside): tech using language religious people perceive as proprietary is "one of the motivators of a lot of religious thinking about tech right now." "It's like, oh, that's my cue."
- **14:04** Wonder and terror are one affect; Hebrew fear of God; Pascal caught between microscope and telescope, "two endless endlessnesses of scale."
- **16:57** Teresa of Avila to her nuns: when you feel worthless, remember Christ died for you; when you feel great, "Remember that you killed Jesus." Kierkegaard: life caught between possibility and necessity; you need both, "you can't make dinner if you're out on Alpha Centauri."
- **19:22** Simcha Bunim's two pockets: "The world was created for me" and "I am dust and ashes." Kalman links it to fast and slow thinking.
- **19:51** Rubenstein: the two modes collide when a child asks "what is God?" and you must answer instantly and well.
- **24:01** Private space industry as the colonial playbook: public-private partnership, occupy, extract, move on. "Oh my gosh, we're doing it again."
- **26:45** Archival Abernathy at Apollo 11: what we can do for space "we must do... for starving poor people."
- **27:50** NASA used promised-land and manifest-destiny language in the 1960s, so religious leaders could unmask it. Now the religious language comes from private CEOs, and the churches "don't have the wherewithal to respond." The Pope, the Ecumenical Patriarch and the Archbishop of Canterbury jointly condemned corporate greed driving climate change "and not a single person knows about it." (Check the joint statement; it is likely the 2021 joint message on climate.)
- **31:08** Navajo Nation objected to human remains sent to the moon as desecration of a sacred body; public response was hostile. A case of a religious community objecting to a "seemingly secular" tech act and being dismissed as backward. (Check details: this was the Peregrine lander, January 2024.)
- **33:21** The simulation hypothesis is "the basic idea of monotheism... just sort of updated with computers"; a superior intelligence "kind of like humanity but better" who "could pull the plug on creation at any time." Then the ethic: keep the simulators entertained, invest in becoming simulators ourselves, and the poor "don't exist" because they are simulated. Her verdict: "philosophically totally uninteresting, ethically disastrous." (Her attribution of this ethic to "the effective altruist line" is polemical and conflates EA with a fringe longtermist reading; would need checking before repeating.)
- **36:21** Kalman's closing confession: religion is "one means among many" for navigating core human instincts, and it "might actually learn things about itself that it had long ago forgotten" through the encounter with tech. Useful for pitching the talk as mutual: alignment labs can learn from religion, and religion can rediscover what its practices were for.

## Tension or objection

- Rubenstein's hermeneutic of suspicion toward messianic tech language applies to the talk itself. A listener could say that calling religion "the oldest alignment lab" is one more case of lofty religious framing laid over an industry with economic motives. Carl should be ready to say what the frame is for and what it is not claiming.
- Her claim that religion is an arbitrary category with no essence complicates saying "religion" did anything in particular across millennia; the talk is on safer ground naming specific practices (catechesis, confession, costly commitment) than "religion" as such.
- Kalman's own preamble that religion is one means among many and not unique undercuts any suggestion that religious formation is the privileged source for alignment. The talk should claim priority of experience (oldest, longest-running), not monopoly.
