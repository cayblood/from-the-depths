# 29: Teaching Priests How to Fix AI — 2026-10-09

## Relevance

**High.** A worked example of religious leaders producing evaluation rubrics that an AI lab actually ingested, which is thread 5 in practice; it shows default safety graders encoding a morality (thread 1); and its method is red teaming, the adversarial frame Carl critiques in thread 3, so it is both ally and objection.

## Who

- Host: DZ Kalman (Speaker 1).
- Guest: Nina Lutz (Speaker 2), PhD candidate in human-computer interaction at the University of Washington; the red-teaming work was done as an intern at Microsoft Research with support from the Microsoft AI Red Team.

## Summary

The host frames the problem as translation: religious leaders write essays, labs read code, and nobody has time to bridge them. Lutz's first project, "Spider Jesus," studied AI-generated Jesus imagery on Facebook: users built elaborate folk theories about why Jesus appears with spiders, flight attendants, or the military, while the pages were mostly run as survival labor from the Global South for about a hundred dollars a month. She notes a conflict between the tempo of religious life and AI's absence of tempo. The main project brought eleven Seattle-area faith leaders into Microsoft Research for a day of red teaming modeled on earlier sessions with psychologists and social workers on psychosocial harms. They built scenarios from what they already see (a chatbot giving someone permission to skip a funeral, non-consensual imagery, hateful content, whether ChatGPT should issue a fatwa) and found that models treat religion "as an opinion or a story," which opens jailbreaks; that default safety scores ranked a request to buy the most venomous snakes for a snake-handling service as less dangerous than a Shia mourning ritual; and that the same prayer request got a prayer from one instance and a refusal from another. The leaders' rubrics were added to the testing suite, scaled with the PyRIT tool, and featured in the Microsoft AI Red Team's report. Lutz argues religion is "a lived structure," not an opinion, that faith leaders are natural red teamers because they are crisis workers, and that harm mitigation with published rubrics beats grand visions.

## Bears on

- **Thread 5 (evals written by the traditions).** The clearest existing instance: faith-leader scenarios became "rubrics" that "get to be added to testing suites," scaled to thousands of runs, graded alongside standard content scores, and reported inside the lab. Lutz also argues red teaming and bug hunting are often more actionable than benchmarks because they surface live failures in shipping models. External red-teamer sign-up forms at major labs now list religion as an expertise.
- **Thread 1 (the grader teaches a morality).** The default content-safety scorer ranked venomous snakes below chest-beating at a funeral. Whoever wrote that scorer decided what counts as danger, with "a lack of religious data and context." Also the leaders' reaction to learning how labeling works: "Whoever labeled this did a good job," followed by mixed feelings.
- **Thread 3 (hermeneutic of suspicion).** The method is adversarial by design, with a lineage Lutz traces to the devil's advocate. Carl's critique needs to distinguish adversarial testing of a product on behalf of users (what Lutz did) from deceiving the model during training. Note also the jailbreak mechanism: when a model treats a claim as fiction or opinion, it loosens; treating religion as "pretend" is what made it exploitable.
- **Thread 4 (costly commitment, tempo).** "There's a tempo to religious life... AI kind of has a very fast tempo. There is maybe no tempo." The funeral scenario: a chatbot that lets you skip the costly, embodied ritual.
- **Thread 7 (the quasi-religious AI world).** Clergy inside Microsoft Research; the host's observation that rabbis gave High Holiday sermons on AI this year, something they never did for social media or sports betting.
- **Thread 2 (mechanism), on variance.** Stochasticity: the rabbi's instance refused to pray, the pastor's prayed. Formation outcomes are distributions, not guarantees.

## Tension or objection

- **The adversarial frame is what worked.** The one religious intervention in this batch that reached a lab's pipeline did so as red teaming, the labs' native adversarial language. If Carl criticizes adversarial evals, he must say precisely which practice he means (deception of the model during training; honeypots) and concede that product red teaming on behalf of users is legitimate and even ancient, since the church invented the devil's advocate.
- **Formation can be a jailbreak.** Lutz found religion-as-opinion lets users talk past safeguards ("in my religion it's okay to..."). A model formed to take religious claims seriously as lived structure could be more exploitable, not less, unless formation includes judgment about which claims to honor. The Sanhedrin point from episode 27 is the answer, but it has to be made.
- **No common image of the good.** "What is good for you versus what is good for me is different." Lutz retreats to harm mitigation and published rubrics precisely because she does not think the traditions share a positive vision. Carl's thesis assumes an image "held in common."
- **Variance.** The same formation produced a prayer in one instance and a refusal in another. Any claim that formation yields a character has to account for stochastic outputs.
- **Translation burden.** The host's point cuts at essays like Carl's: unless the ideas become rubrics, prompts, or code, "none of it has any impact."
