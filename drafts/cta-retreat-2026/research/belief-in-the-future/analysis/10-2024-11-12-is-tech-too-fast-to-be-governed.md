# 10 — Is Tech Too Fast to Be Governed? (2024-11-12)

## Relevance

**Low.** A governance conversation with no religious or alignment content; it offers two transferable frames (laws as principles with latitude versus prescriptive rules, and deliberation versus adversarial process) and one good anecdote about a rabbi exhausted by technology questions in 1900.

## Who

- **Host:** David Zvi Kalman (transcript: "Davit Sv. Kallman"), Speaker 1.
- **Guest:** Gideon Lichfield (transcript: "Gideon Litchfield"), journalist; formerly at The Economist, editor-in-chief of MIT Technology Review and then of Wired; now writing the newsletter Futuropolis on governance and technology. Speaker 2. Interview recorded before the November 2024 US election.

## Summary

Kalman frames governments and organized religions as parallel slow institutions whose value proposition is permanence, both run by people with limited tech literacy, both reactive, both asking whether to resist technological change or adapt it to their purposes. Lichfield argues that our governance systems predate the railway and telegraph and asks what decision-making would look like if designed for the present; he cites a Tokyo gubernatorial candidate who used social media and an AI avatar to refine a manifesto. The core mismatch is that laws take years to make and then are set in stone while technology iterates. He relays Marietje Schaake's proposal (from *The Tech Coup*): write laws around principles and rights, give agencies latitude and the duty to update, and fund enforcement. To keep that democratic, he favors citizens' assemblies: randomly selected representative panels, briefed by experts, facilitated toward consensus rather than partisan votes, as in Ireland. Kalman mourns the lost intermediary institutions, religion among them, where such deliberation once happened organically; Lichfield replies that those institutions still exist but their moderating power has been diluted by everyone having a bullhorn. On speed, Lichfield says the late nineteenth century was equally wrenching and produced unions, child labor laws and antitrust; speed cannot be slowed, so institutions must be "updated constantly." On bad actors, the new problem is multi-use technologies whose uses cannot be predicted; enforcement must be invented from scratch, "an arms race." They end on science fiction's poverty of imagination about governance, America's quasi-religious reverence for its Constitution, and local government as the lab for democratic experiment.

## Bears on

- **Thread 1 (rules versus formation):** Schaake's argument at 09:31 is an institutional analogue of Carl's. Prescriptive laws aimed at today's technology "become quickly outdated"; better to encode principles and "leave the interpretation of those principles down to lower level bodies." A rulebook ages; formed judgment with a mandate to keep learning does not.
- **Thread 3 (adversarial frame):** Citizens' assemblies exist "to get away from the adversarial model, which defines the way parliaments work, and instead have a deliberation where people can find common ground" (12:09). Transparency of the deliberation builds trust (15:04). A governance echo of D&C 121's persuasion over compulsion.
- **Thread 7 (quasi-religious culture):** Kalman names religious institutions as the places "larger than the family but smaller than the state" where people once formed views organically (16:26); Lichfield: the old institutions "moderated the voices" and new assemblies are "recreating another version of that" (17:07). America treats its Constitution as "this almost religious document that one cannot possibly change" (32:55).
- **General framing of the talk:** "institutions and laws and norms are not things that we create and then leave to run. They have to be updated constantly... There's never a day when we can say, All right, we fixed it" (22:32). Alignment as ongoing formation, not a one-time fix.

## Tension or objection

- Lichfield's position that the pace cannot be slowed and that the correct response is "an arms race" of enforcement is the governance version of the adversarial frame; he sees no alternative. Carl's claim that patient formation can move the needle has to answer "at what speed?"
- Kalman's preamble that religion "isn't particularly unique" as a place to learn to be moral, ask big questions or find community (00:00) is the same objection as in episode 07: priority of experience, not monopoly.
- The episode's one explicit treatment of religion-and-tech (24:29) shows the tradition overwhelmed rather than wise: a rabbi with no time to dig through the Talmud. Useful, but it does not flatter the lab.
