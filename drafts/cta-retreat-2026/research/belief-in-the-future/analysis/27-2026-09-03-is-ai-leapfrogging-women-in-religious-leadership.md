## Summary

The guest's starting observation is that "AI's Torah" has been embraced by Jewish communities in a way that women's Torah was not and still is not. The suspicion directed at women who wanted to learn (what is motivating you?) was never directed at AI, whose motives actually do involve corporate incentives. She traces the double standard partly to embodiment: the tradition is suspicious of women's bodies, and the image of God is invoked definitionally to mark humans off from machines in a way that, oddly, constrains the beings who have it. The AI that never sleeps is a Maimonidean fantasy of pure intellect, and metaphors run both ways: calling computers brains made us think of brains as computers. Against the "package delivery" model of knowledge she argues humans are an underrated delivery system; who teaches you changes what you learn. The host worries AI will cheapen argument and calcify norms; she answers with the Talmud's Sanhedrin qualification. The last third covers Sefaria's library assistant (built to send you into the sources, not to keep you talking), an AI-assisted translation of the Kli Yakar reviewed word by word by a human, and translation as always both loss and gain.

## Tension or objection

- **Imago Dei as a wall.** Carl's "image in the countenance" language will meet religious listeners who use the image of God precisely to deny that a machine can bear it. The guest's point, that the definitional move does hidden work, is both a warning and a resource.
- **Whose image of the good?** If religion is the alignment lab, the traditions that write the evals have their own exclusions. A talk about an image held in common should say who got to hold it.
- **Cheap argument.** The host's worry that AI makes every argument free, so none persuades, applies to tradition-written evals too: a model can satisfy any rubric. The Sanhedrin answer (wisdom, not capability) is the way through, but it moves the burden to character, which is harder to evaluate.
