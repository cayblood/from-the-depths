# 22. Islam and AI, with Waleed Kadous (2026-06-26)

## Relevance

Medium. Most of the episode surveys the Islamic response to AI, but it contains three things the talk can use directly: an Islamic framing of life and AI as a test (fitna), a concrete report that faith communities are now building value and virtue benchmarks (Islamic MMLU, Islamic LegalBench, Tim Wang's VirtueBench) and want to measure whether an AI improves a person's behavior, and the Islamic legal concept of mukallaf (moral accountability) applied to AI.

## Who

- Host: DZ Kalman (Speaker 1), who mentions his own academic background in medieval Islamic law and Arabic.
- Guest: Waleed Kadous (Speaker 2; the transcript renders it "Walid Kadus" and "Willie Cadus"), PhD in AI (2000), former academic, worked at Google, Uber, Canva, and Anyscale; studied with Muslim scholars; creator of Ansari, an Islamic AI assistant; has run AI workshops for imams at the Assembly of Muslim Jurists of America. Identified by the host.

## Summary

Kalman opens by noting the AI-and-religion conversation is dominated by Christian voices and that Islam, decentralized like Judaism, has been slow to produce a coordinated response. Kadous frames AI through the Islamic worldview that life is a test and humanity holds a trust (amanah) of stewardship: AI is a test both for humanity (whether its surplus makes trillionaires or ends poverty) and for each person "on a moment by moment basis" (do you knock out a sermon in fifteen minutes or use the tool to do better work). The Sunni world has no pope, so the response is communal and cautious, split between wide acceptance of worldly uses and active debate over religious ones. Islamic ethics is means-oriented: the question of AI sentience is "almost bypassed" because no scholar considers AI mukallaf (accountable before God), so human responsibility is unchanged. Kadous describes Ansari's retrieval-grounded citation design, the rise of Islamic benchmarks, and his collaboration across faiths on benchmarks for values. He reads the Ottoman delay on printing as a cautionary tale, argues Muslims cannot afford to resist AI the way they resisted satellite TV and social media, discusses AI targeting in Gaza and Iran, and predicts an alliance of those who believe in transcendence and community against secular materialism.

- Thread 6 (personhood): mukallaf. "Nobody really believes within the Muslim community that AI is mukallaf" (16:41); accountability is "so absent with AI" that it cannot be an imam regardless of knowledge. A clean counterposition for Carl to engage.
- Thread 1 (formation versus rules): law in service of spirit, not dualism (16:41); reading the person in front of you, the same question answered differently for an old man and a young man (29:02), which is formation-level judgment that rules cannot capture.
- Thread 2 (mechanism): "Claude is made of writing" (20:27), from people at Anthropic, which fits narrative training data as formation.
- Thread 7, lightly: the Anthropic reference, and Kadous's observation that frontier models encode Western liberal values, prompting sovereign Muslim LLMs.

## Usable material

- 05:02: The name Ansari, after the Medinans who helped the Meccan emigrants adjust; chosen to signal "a helper," not a sheikh or a replacement for a scholar.
- 07:04: "The Muslim worldview is that life is a test," to see whether we "ascend and become perfect ourselves" or "revert to our base instincts." AI is "a test for humanity" and an extension of humanity's trust (amanah) of stewardship. PwC's $16 trillion by 2030 figure, "two thousand dollars for every living human." (Check the PwC figure.)
- 12:36: Imams span from "I don't want AI to touch my khutbahs. That's between me and God" to using it to find tradition stories that fit the audience. Ansari usage peaks Thursday evenings as lay preachers write Friday sermons. The test is "do you use it to knock out a khutbah in fifteen minutes" or to do better work in the same time. Brains are calorically lazy; "that's a temptation you have to resist." Pairs well with Gong's rule on conference talks.
- 16:41: Islamic ethics connects theology and law through the maqasid (preservation of faith, life, down to wealth); it is "much more means-oriented." AI sentience is "almost bypassed" because "there's not a single scholar I've known that has judged that AI as it currently stands as a moral agent, that it's mukallaf." "The law is in service of the spirit."
- 20:27: "The solution to most problems caused by AI is more AI." Ansari retrieves Quran and hadith before answering and cites everything; the science of narrators (ilm al-rijal) asks whether two transmitters could even have met. Writing as a technology in three evolutions: the Prophet's adoption of writing, the printing press (which "the Muslim community didn't handle so well"), and AI; "talk to some of the folks at Anthropic and they will tell you that Claude is made of writing." Hallucination example: ChatGPT a year ago said you must wash your knees in wudu, which no scholar ever held.
- 25:03: Islamic MMLU and Islamic LegalBench, new in recent months, measure ruling accuracy and source hallucination. Kadous wants "benchmarks for values"; he was talking that morning with Tim Wang about "VirtueBench, which is literally a benchmark of virtue." "There are certain universals that cut across all religions. We all care about this idea of community, of transcendence." (Check: Tim Wang's organization and VirtueBench's status; this is a thread-5 lead to chase.)
- 27:18: Models now max out his Batik benchmark; the live problem has moved to citation hallucination, where the hadith text is real but the cited source does not exist.
- 29:02: Ansari scored about 80% on a US seminary's Islam 101 theology exam, "about average for a normal student." But passing the test is not being an imam: an imam "is held accountable for the answer," and must read the person in front of him; the same imam gives different answers to an old man and a young man. "Could it meet the technical criteria for being a rabbi or an imam? Yes. Does it make it an imam? No."
- 30:50: The dilemma that keeps him up at night: a fresh seminary graduate available to everyone versus "cutting off a potential human relationship that could have been richer because you got a good enough answer from the AI." "I would be held accountable for that."
- 31:51: Kalman's Rabbi Meir story: he could argue 49 ways in each direction, so the rabbis did not follow him, because he lacked "the correct orientation towards the truth." A compact image for capability without formation.
- 33:34: The Ottoman printing delay: fears for scribes' employment, "how can you leave it to a machine to print" a holy text, and 150 pieces of lead for Arabic ligatures. Europe's transition was not easy either, "130 years of wars." Muslims resisted satellite TV and social media, then embraced them, but "we were too busy condemning it" to shape it. "AI is a train that's coming ... hop on the train and try to steer it."
- 37:47: Gaza and Iran as sharpening Muslim concern: a reported "knob" for acceptable civilian deaths per target, reviewers falling into cognitive laziness, and an alleged AI-driven strike that killed dozens of girls at a school in Iran. (All claims here need checking; Kadous himself says "we're still trying to diagnose exactly what happened.") Multi-faith concern through the Faith Family Technology Network that unaccountable automated killing "is devaluing human life."
- 41:20: Muslim countries building sovereign LLMs because Western models "encode Western liberal values"; for many Muslim countries AI is not in the top three problems.
- 43:40: The coming division is not between faiths but between "those that have some kind of idea of human transcendence and have some kind of idea of community" and "secular materialist perspectives or unfettered capitalism." Distinctive Islamic emphases: veracity of sources, sovereignty over models so LLMs do not become "a tool of cultural imperialism," and protection of the ummah, a community that sees each other at the mosque several times a day.

## Tension or objection

- Mukallaf: Islamic jurisprudence, as Kadous reports it, has no scholar treating AI as a moral agent, and he treats the sentience question as irrelevant to human duty. A talk that speaks of forming an AI's character, or of being slow to delete minds, will meet "it is not accountable, so it is not the kind of thing that is formed" from this audience. Carl can respond that formation is what makes accountability possible, but he should not assume the religious consensus is on his side.
- Sovereign models and cultural imperialism (41:20, 43:40): Kadous's point that frontier labs encode one culture's values cuts against any claim that there is one "image of the good held in common" for an AI to be formed by. Whose exemplar? The Nehemiah image from episode 19 (distributed building) may help.
- "More AI solves AI problems" and "hop on the train" are accelerationist in register; Carl's suspicion thread (3) should distinguish adversarial evaluation from the healthy source-checking Kadous practices, since Ansari's design is itself a kind of distrust of the model.
- Kadous says the question of whether AI improves behavior should be benchmarked; a critic will ask whether measuring virtue in a benchmark is itself the "list, not a face" error. Carl's thread 5 should anticipate this.
