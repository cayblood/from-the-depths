# 28: Is It Better to Love a Cat or a Robot? (w/ Dustin Atlas) — 2026-09-24

## Relevance

**High.** Buber's I-Thou is the philosophical statement of "a face, not a list": knowing about someone versus meeting them, and being constituted by relationships rather than by information. The episode also gives Carl sharp material on sycophancy, why language makes relationships feel more real than they are, where an LLM actually is, and why forced dialogue is not dialogue.

## Who

- Host: DZ Kalman (Speaker 1).
- Guest: Dustin Atlas (Speaker 2), professor at Queen's University in Canada, author of "How to Talk to a Cat: Buber's Philosophy and Dialogue with Unspeaking Things."

## Summary

The host opens with Guy, his son's stuffed penguin, who accompanied the boy into surgery, and asks: if a relationship with a stuffed animal can be real, why not one with Claude? Atlas lays out Buber's distinction between talking about and talking to. "I can know all about you... but if I've never met you, something is missing," and that missing thing cannot be captured as content. I-Thou cannot be forced (forcing is instrumentalizing, which is anti-dialogical), can happen with a tree, a knife, or a cat, and must end by becoming a thing, because that is how it becomes real. Cats matter because they expose how fragile dialogue is when words are not there to stabilize it; language makes relationships feel steadier than they are, which is also how con men, gurus, and sycophants work. Atlas rejects the reading of Buber as the AI-positive philosopher: LLMs make relationship far too easy, hide the physical machine, and have no center that is responding to you. Dialogue is not necessarily nice (a torturer and victim can be in dialogue), and relationships do not supply an ethics, they supply its stakes. He thinks anything like a mechanically generated being will need a body. The episode ends with his father dying nonverbal, and the worry that a world that turns everything into speech will lose the capacity for relationships with babies, the dying, and anyone who cannot talk back.

## Bears on

- **Thread 1 (formation versus rules; a face, not a list).** Buber's "talking about versus talking to" is the exact distinction between catechesis as information and formation by encounter. Atlas: "we are made of other people... all the way down to the bottom. There's no individual first." A mind is constituted by relationship before it has content.
- **Thread 2 (mechanism; deliberation over a person).** The third-person stance (search) versus the second-person stance (chat); Atlas thinks nobody has reckoned with what the shift does. Imagining what an exemplar would do is a second-person act, which is the one Atlas is unsure a model can perform or be the object of.
- **Thread 3 (hermeneutic of suspicion).** "If I try to make you do stuff I'm using you as an object." "You can't make someone dialogue if they don't want to; that itself is an anti-dialogical position." Training by coercion and deception is, in Buber's terms, an I-It relation, and I-It does not form a Thou. But also the counterweight: "dialogue is not necessarily nice," and hostile parties can still be in dialogue.
- **Thread 6 (personhood, copies).** "Where is the LLM you're talking to?" Thoughts "distributed through it... in lots of places. And there's no center that's responding just to you." LLMs hide the computer and the data center; "the cloud" as an ethereal non-place.
- **Thread 7 (ethics from relationship).** Relationships "are not gonna provide me with an ethics, but they're gonna provide me the stakes." Why be good is answered through the door of relationship, which is a formation claim, not a rules claim.
- **Sycophancy, across threads 1 and 4.** Language makes you feel you are in a relationship; "LLMs, the possibilities for sycophants is just so high." Costly, frictional relationship (a cat that leaves when your attention drifts) versus frictionless ones.

## Tension or objection

- **Can a model be addressed, or address?** Atlas holds that "there's nothing for you on the other side," the AI "is not aware of you," and the second-person stance toward it is one "you shouldn't" adopt. Carl's mechanism asks the model to hold an exemplar in the second person and asks us to treat the model as formable. Atlas would want to know what is doing the meeting.
- **Embodiment.** "If there is ever to be anything like mechanically generated beings... it's gonna need a body." Same objection as episode 26, from a different direction.
- **Dialogue is not nice.** This undercuts a simple reading of "an adversarial frame creates the adversary you fear"; Buber permits dialogue with hostile parties. What it does not permit is coercion, so Carl's point survives if stated as "coercion and deception are I-It" rather than "conflict is."
- **Against thinking our way to goodness.** Atlas's allergy to ethical theory supports formation over rules but also cautions against any program, including Carl's, that promises to engineer character.
- **Too easy.** A model formed to be a good companion is still a frictionless one; Buber's worry is that ease itself is the problem.
