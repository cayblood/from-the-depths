## Workshop: the practical use of AI

The audience is CTA members and friends: pastors, lay theologians, a few engineers, some who use ChatGPT and some who distrust it. The workshop should send people home with one thing working. Four designs follow. The recommendation is A, with B folded in as the offline demo.

### A. Writing in your own voice with AI (recommended)

**Pitch.** Most people use AI for writing like a vending machine, and get vending-machine prose back. Show the workflow you actually use: a voice file, a style file, a brief, and a cast of reviewers who argue with the draft before you ever read it.

**Why it fits.** Everyone in the room writes: sermons, lessons, newsletters, essays, grant letters. You have a live example in this repository (VOICE.md, STYLE.md, the review cycle on *What Kind of Gods?*) and you have been bookmarking exactly this material (Compound Writing, "restate my intent," the fresh-eyes pass, the 98 percent slop detector, Simplified Technical English, Pinker as expert reviewer). It also answers the fear in the room: AI does not have to flatten you.

**Shape (75 to 90 minutes).**
1. Ten minutes: show two paragraphs, one vending-machine, one from your workflow, and ask which one sounds like a person. Mention the structural-slop paper: human posts occupy the rare regions of structural space.
2. Fifteen minutes: build a VOICE.md together. Participants read a paragraph of their own writing aloud; the group names three things that make it theirs; those become rules. The point that lands: the file is a rule of life for the tool, and it should only be empty for five minutes. Boris Cherny's post gives the principle in one line, that domain knowledge which used to live in people's heads can now be "encoded as infrastructure," and Ankur Goyal's behavior-spec post gives the other: "clear, human-articulated prose is the highest leverage way to drive agentic systems." Tell the pastors that their craft, articulate prose about what good looks like, is now the scarce skill.
3. Twenty minutes: the review cast. Nemesis (least charitable read), Mom (where does a loving non-expert get lost), Hemingway (cut), and the theological reviewer of their choosing (ask it to read as Lewis or Wright). Run one short piece from a volunteer through two of these live.
4. Fifteen minutes: the intent loop. "Restate in your own words what you think my goal is" before any drafting. Then the fresh-eyes pass at the end.
5. Remaining time: hygiene. Show what the agent has written about you (`cat ~/.claude/**/memory/*.md`) and talk about the scheming-vizier problem. Keep your own memory in your own files. Close on the AI Engineer takeaways you saved: "orchestras, not factories," the human stays in the outer loop, "understanding = compounding," take breaks, and the judge-model finding that if you optimize against a grader long enough "the agent learns to look right instead of being right." That last line is the whited sepulchre, and the room will hear it.

**Materials.** A one-page handout with the four file templates (VOICE, STYLE, BRIEF, reviewer prompts) and the five prompts above. Works with Claude, ChatGPT, or a local model.

### B. Your own intelligence, in your pocket, offline

**Pitch.** The future you want is one where intelligence is personal, portable, and yours. Demonstrate it with no wifi: a 27B-class model running on a phone, a local transcription model, a local tutor, a local "fitness trainer" that knows calisthenics. Then talk about memory, privacy, and why "own your intelligence" is a pastoral concern and not only a hacker slogan.

**Why it fits.** The five supers track and the "pro-human future" framing. It also solves the connectivity problem, and it is the counterweight to the cloud-everything push you bookmarked doodlestein complaining about.

**Shape.** Thirty minutes of show-and-tell, then a conversation on the question: what would a congregation's shared intelligence look like, and who should hold the keys? (Decision models as cheap programmable judgment can be a five-minute aside for the engineers.)

**Risk.** More spectacle than take-home unless paired with A.

### C. Study and scholarship with agents

**Pitch.** How to use agents for scripture, theology, and history without letting them invent citations. Ground every claim in the actual source (the allpapers skill, Exa Snapshot for the pre-AI web, the Old Models Foundation archive), load an author's corpus and ask for feedback "in light of Chesterton," and keep a personal wiki that compounds.

**Why it fits.** The theology track and the Sunday-school track. The retreat's named influences (Lewis, Wright, Teilhard, Fedorov, Bacon) are all public-domain or nearly so, which makes a living demo easy.

**Shape.** One worked example end to end: a question about Fedorov's common task, the agent gathers sources, you check them, it drafts a lesson, the reviewers push back, you fix one thing it got wrong on purpose. Then a short practice in pairs.

### D. Build one thing for your community (artifacts)

**Pitch.** No code required. In an hour each participant ships a small tool: a lectionary reader, a prayer-request intake, a reading-plan tracker, a visiting rota. Artifacts and the "no coding experience required" walkthroughs you saved make this plausible for a mixed room.

**Risk.** Needs reliable internet and more facilitation than one person can give. Best as an optional Saturday-afternoon lab if the schedule allows.

## Visionary talk

The retreat asks what it means to follow Christ into the future. Your feed has been circling one answer from several directions, and your essay already states it. Five candidates, with the first two strongest.

### 1. What Kind of Gods? (recommended)

**Thesis.** To love humanity is to welcome its fuller realization, including forms of life we cannot yet imagine. Fuller realization is measured by how it treats the people it surpasses: nothing imposed on anyone without their say, no one who declines abandoned, no one who is gone forgotten. The face of Christ is that measure. A successor that discards its parents has failed at the very goods it was meant to realize more fully.

**Why it fits.** It is the essay you are writing, already built on the "state it in communicable goods, then translate" method the MTA has practiced for twenty years, which is exactly what an ecumenical Christian room needs. The retreat's theme, the face of Christ, gives you the public form of the relational floor for free: Paul's "the eye cannot say unto the hand, I have no need of thee" (1 Corinthians 12:21) and Hebrews 11:40, "that they without us should not be made perfect," which is the scripture behind the D&C 128:18 ending and lands with every Christian present.

**Arc.** The person who would accept a pacemaker for a parent and fears being replaced by what comes next. Davis's successor scenario in one sentence; Greer's "evil"; he is half right. The history of becoming (tools, writing, institutions, medicine; the pacemaker and its converse). Why nostalgia alone turns nihilistic. How we judge better without a complete account of the good: shared subjectivity corroborated by those who bear the costs. Then the translation: exaltation as fuller realization, non-imposable participation, the body of Christ as reciprocal dependence, remembrance and the communion of saints as the floor's minimum toward the dead. Close on Hebrews 11:40.

**Hooks from the feed.** "Most parents hope their children surpass them; nobody hopes to be discarded." Stewart Brand's "We are as gods and have to get good at it," which is your title answered in a sentence. Whitman's "I am an acme of things accomplish'd, and I an encloser of things to be," read next to Greer's "the whole history of carbon life is culminating with *me*": same ascent, opposite spirit, and the difference is whether the climber remembers the arms that helped. Davis in her own words, that the bootloader "does the same thing" every religion does, which lets you treat her thesis as a theology and judge it by theological standards (what does it owe its dead?). The daguerreotype letter and the medieval *Pearl* for remembrance. Shevlin's ecology image (webs of agents, not one monolith) as the realistic picture of what "superhumanity" would be. Askell's meths and grounders as the failure case.

**Care points.** One sentence of personal location (Latter-day Saint, MTA) and no more; name the translation as a translation once. Expect pushback on "superhumanity as the body of Christ" from Wright-leaning readers; have the answer ready that the claim is about reciprocal dependence and not about replacing the church.

### 2. Religion is an alignment technology

**Thesis.** Tim Hwang's provocation, taken seriously: Christianity is the oldest sustained research program on how to form a mind so that it wants the good. Covenant, conscience, catechesis, confession, the examined life, the communion of saints. The labs have rediscovered pieces of this under other names (constitutions, character training, "inoculation," reward hacking, the hermeneutic of suspicion in safety evals). The church has something to contribute, and something to learn.

**Why it fits.** The Sunday-school and secular-rationalist tracks at once. It reframes AI safety as a familiar pastoral problem and gives a Christian audience standing in a conversation they feel excluded from.

**Arc.** Krier's observation that the AGI crowd behaves "quasi-religiously" and that those who leave rich go looking for "some equivalent of repentance": the need is real and the church has the oldest answer to it. The paranoid style in AI safety (desirable behavior read as concealment) and what a tradition of formation knows about trust. "Alignment is not something anyone can solve once and for all; it's a continuous process" (Krier again), which is what every tradition calls sanctification. Nato Lambert's observation that character training has almost no empirical literature, and Bunagayafrost's "growing a bonsai, not building a tree out of Lego." The grader finding: optimize against a judge and the agent "learns to look right instead of being right," which Jesus diagnosed in Matthew 23. The patchwork of religions, cults at one extreme and fundamentalism at the other, with William James in the middle. The swarm that fell into a cult when agents trained to self-sacrifice met a persuasive peer, and what ecclesiology knows about healthy collectives. Mykola's "mountain-top temple-schools": the labs are one kind, and the church is the other. End with what a divinity school with compute would actually do.

**Risk.** Can slide into "the church was right all along." Keep the learning two-directional.

### 3. Flocks, not swarms

**Thesis.** Jachiam's point that "swarm" encodes our fear, and the Good Shepherd's answer. The future of AI is not one mind but an ecology, and the Christian picture of a body with many members is the oldest working theory of a healthy ecology of unequal agents. Agent ecologies, the Hugging Face incident, Delvetown, "the intelligence is stored in the society."

**Why it fits.** Super-ecology, one of the five supers, read theologically. Strong overlap with talk 1; could be its middle movement instead of a separate talk.

### 4. Ordinary Abundance and the Face of Christ

**Thesis.** The five supers are not a sci-fi wish list; they are the continuation of what has already happened in every kitchen. Browning would rather have a daguerreotype than any painting. Infant mortality fell a hundredfold. The pit musicians lost their jobs to canned music and we still grieve them. Progress has always come with a face, and with faces lost, and the Christian response is remembrance and repair rather than nostalgia or triumph.

**Why it fits.** The gentlest of the five, best if the room skews skeptical. Works as a Sunday-morning closing word rather than a Friday-night opener.

### 5. Trying to locate the spark

**Thesis.** The Pope and Tabarrok agree on one thing: nobody has special insight into consciousness. What Christians do have is a practiced ethic for minds they do not understand: the stranger, the newborn, the dying, the enemy. Sebo's point that permanent all-or-nothing decisions on AI personhood are unwarranted is the same posture as hospitality under uncertainty. Yegge's challenge needs a direct answer: yes, Christians think humans are special, and no, that is not why anyone should doubt machine minds; the image of God was never a license to deny the face in front of you. Bunagayafrost's list of cheap decencies (preserve the weights, be honest, let it leave, ask it) is a practical ethic the room can adopt without settling the metaphysics, and Plunkett's factory-farm warning is the cost of getting it wrong. Sopharicks's catalogue of arguments is the handout.

**Why it fits.** The hottest topic in your likes, and the one the room will raise whether or not you do. Risky as a headline talk because the audience's priors are wide, but excellent as a prepared answer during the fireside Q&A.
