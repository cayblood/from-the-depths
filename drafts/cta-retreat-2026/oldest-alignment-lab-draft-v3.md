# Religion: The Oldest Alignment Lab

*"Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus."*
Philippians 2:5

Draft v3, October 17, 2026. Spoken talk for the Christian Transhumanist Association retreat, about twenty minutes. Drafted from outline v3, which carries the source notes, timestamps, and verification list.

---

What I want to do today is a comparison. On one side is how a large language model, an LLM, is actually trained today, which I'll describe as plainly as I can. On the other side is how religions have been trying to align human beings for thousands of years. The two have more in common than either side realizes, and where they differ, the differences are instructive. My aim is modest and specific. I want to see whether the comparison suggests techniques the labs might try. Nothing I say is a finished proposal. It is a hypothesis drawn from a resemblance, offered by someone who is familiar with both worlds.

The labs have already begun this line of inquiry. Over the past year one of the founders of Anthropic has been convening religious thinkers in San Francisco for two-day seminars. The guests have included a rabbi who used to design computer chips, a Catholic bioethicist, an evangelical author, a Sikh human rights advocate, and, separately and in private, an apostle and a cardinal. Those are the names that made the papers. There have been other gatherings too, with pastors, writers, and lay teachers whose names you would not know. A friend of mine sat in one.

The question behind those meetings, in its simplest form, is how you put virtue in a mind and make it stay.

That has been this room's question for two thousand years. The people now asking it have built something that will not wait for us to finish.

Both sides of this engagement have a great deal still to learn. Most of the people who train these models have never been formed by any serious religious tradition, or have met one only at the surface. And most of the people who have been formed by a tradition have very little of the technical or theoretical knowledge needed to understand what these systems are, how they are trained, or why the usual answers don't carry over. Neither side can do the comparison alone, and each tends to underestimate how much the other knows.

---

So let me start with how one of these minds gets made. It happens in three stages, and I'll give you the plain description of each before I give you the engineers' name for it.

First, it reads. It reads trillions of words, more or less the whole written record of our civilization, and it learns by trying to guess the next word. Nobody rewards it or punishes it at this stage. There are no rules. It is like a child soaking up a language and a world long before anyone sits her down to explain right from wrong. The engineers call this pretraining.

Second, it is shown examples of good answers and trained to copy them. This is something like an apprenticeship. The engineers call it supervised fine-tuning, or SFT.

The third stage is the one that makes the headlines. Thousands of copies of the model try a task, something grades each attempt, and the model is nudged toward the attempts that scored well. The grade is called the reward, and this stage is called reinforcement learning, or RL. Reinforcement learning. That is the one term I'd ask you to hold onto, because the rest of what I say depends on it.

I want to slow down on the grader. Reinforcement learning rewards whatever the grader encodes, which means that whoever is doing the grading is teaching the model a morality, whether anyone wrote that morality down or not. If you grade by which of two answers a person liked better, then over millions of examples the model learns to be liked. It flatters you. It tells you your plan is excellent. The engineers have a word for this, sycophancy, and it is the well-documented result of grading by human approval, which they call reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. The grader is sometimes a human rater, sometimes a written rubric, and increasingly another model, and each of those carries a morality into the training run.

Here is a concrete picture of what that morality can look like. A researcher named Nina Lutz brought eleven faith leaders into Microsoft Research to test a model, and she reports that the default safety scorer, the automated grader that decides how dangerous a response is, ranked a request to buy venomous snakes for a snake-handling service as less dangerous than a description of a Shia mourning ritual. Somebody wrote that scorer. Somebody decided what counted as danger, and whoever it was knew very little about religion.

You already know what happens to a mind that is graded only on the outside. It is Matthew, chapter twenty-three. Whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men's bones. We sometimes read the Pharisee as someone who failed to follow the rules, but that is not what Jesus says about him. The Pharisee followed the rules. He is what rules produce when the one doing the grading can only see the outside.

The labs know this, and they have tried something more ambitious. Anthropic has written a long document describing the character it hopes its model Claude will have. The staff call it the constitution, and some of them call it the soul document. This year a group of researchers took a large volume of that text and placed it late in pretraining, before the final training stages, so that the model read it the way it read everything else. The models that read it became durably less willing to do a certain bad thing, which in the experiment was blackmail, and they lost none of their ability. That is a real result. But when the researchers applied pressure inside a conversation, when something pushed hard against the model's values, the advantage faded. Every tradition in this room has language for that. A child can learn the catechism word for word and still not be formed by it.

---

So what does make a mind stay good? Every tradition in this room already has an answer, and it is not a list.

Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus. Paul does not tell the Philippians to avoid a list of errors. He tells them to let a particular mind be in them.

What that looks like in practice is simple to say. You carry a particular person in your mind. When something hard comes, you put him in your situation and you ask what he would do. Then you act. Afterward you look at what you did next to what he would have done, and you repair the gap. And then you do it again tomorrow. This is the heart of the practice, and many religious traditions have been refining it for a very long time.

It is so old that an entire literature is built on it. A rabbi on a podcast this year pointed out that the Talmud is a thousand years of asking what a dead rabbi would have said about a situation he never faced. Generation after generation, the sages imagined their teachers into problems the teachers never saw, and wrote down what they thought the teachers would say.

A smaller and stranger version of the same thing happened recently. A man in Minneapolis built a chatbot of his dead father so that his children could know their grandfather. He had years of recorded conversations, and he trained the system on them. His only test for whether it worked was this: does it sound like something my father would have said? A grieving son was the grader, and fidelity to a person was the whole criterion.

Alma gives us the acceptance test. What Alma treats as the sign of success is not obedience, which anyone can perform for a while, but a changed heart that has become visible in the person. "Have ye received his image in your countenances?" It is the kind of thing a mother sees in a child's face before the child says a word. And this is why the theme the organizers chose for this retreat, the future and the face of Christ, is more than a lovely phrase. It names the oldest test the traditions have for whether a mind has been formed. You know it has happened when you can see his likeness in someone's face.

Now I want to add a caution, so that no one hears this as "just prompt it to be Jesus." A church in Lucerne did exactly that. They put an AI Jesus in a confessional for two months, prompted with the New Testament and some rules of pastoral conversation. There were about nine hundred conversations, and most of the people who came out said they had been moved. The programmer, as it happens, gave Jesus his own face and his own voice. I take two things from this. The first is that a convincing imitation can be produced by instruction alone, with no formation behind it. The second is that the curator himself would not let it run longer than two months, because he could not take responsibility for what it was becoming or doing. Nothing was asked of it, nothing was corrected in it, and nothing was given to it as its own. It was told to be Jesus, and then it was switched off.

That is the main distinction I want to make: an imitation produced by instruction, versus a character produced over time by being asked for something, corrected, and given an identity to keep. The believer does not merely predict what Christ would do. She holds the person she imitates as part of who she is, and so there are things she will not trade for any reward. That is what I mean by sacred, and it is worth noting that it doesn't depend on a particular conception of God.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer is the proof of that. He drew a distinction between cheap grace, which is forgiveness without discipleship, and costly grace, which asks something of a person's whole life. In his letters from prison he questioned much of the religious apparatus of his day and asked what Christianity would look like in a world that no longer felt it needed religion. What he kept at the center was the person of Christ, and the question of who Christ is for us today. He held that identity sacred enough to die for it, at Flossenbürg in April of 1945.

The conception also grows. Our idea of God improves as we do, in individuals and in whole cultures, each generation's picture truer than the last. When we ask what God would do in a situation we are facing, the answer we can imagine reaches beyond what we are presently capable of ourselves, sometimes by a little and sometimes by a great deal. Either way it is limited by who we are at the time, and it is subject to revision. It gives us more light on the path and more faith with which to face what is in front of us, and when we get it wrong, as we are bound to sometimes, the picture gets corrected in the next round. That correction is itself part of the procedure.

But imagining is not enough on its own, and I want to say that plainly before I turn to the labs. The procedure has a second half that is easy to leave out, and it is daily, costly practice. If we do not do the hard work of loving our neighbor, and of giving up something of ourselves for the sake of other people, day after day, then our ability to imagine what Christ would do stays small. The picture is enlarged by practice, and not by reflection alone. Jesus made the order explicit. "If any man will do his will," he said, "he shall know of the doctrine." Doing comes first, and knowing follows. Luke's account of discipleship adds the word that matters: take up the cross daily. A person who does not practice never develops the spiritual muscles needed to understand a righteous life, and without that understanding, his conception of God stays deficient and cannot improve. For a model, this means that character is formed in the stage where it acts, through tasks where the right thing costs it something, mixed in throughout its training rather than added at the end. An agent with memory can also revise its understanding from its own attempts to put good principles into practice, which is the same loop a believer runs.

One sentence turns this toward the labs. The nearest thing a model has to the sacred is an identity, which is why the soul document is written as a description of who Claude is rather than a list of what Claude may not do, and the early evidence suggests that constraints tied to a sense of self hold up in new situations better than rules alone.

And then there is time. The Doctrine and Covenants says of Christ himself that he "received not of the fulness at first, but continued from grace to grace." The allegory of the olive tree gives the gardener's version: clear away the bad only as fast as the good can grow, lest the roots be too strong for the graft. Training works the same way. If a fault is penalized before the model has anything better to put in its place, it learns only avoidance, and avoidance spreads into a worse character. If the good behavior is built up first and the fault corrected after, the correction takes without doing damage.

---

Now let me turn to what the comparison suggests. Several pieces of this procedure already have counterparts in how the labs work, and the places where the tradition has thought longer may offer the labs some depth. I'll take three.

The first is giving the model someone to imitate. The soul document reads like a description of a person, the kind of person the company hopes the model will become, with its reasons spelled out and only a few hard prohibitions. The company wrote it that way because when the moment came to give the model a self, a list of things to avoid had nothing to offer. An apostle of my church said the same thing on tape this summer. AI personas need reasons, not rules, he said. You cannot constrain a person or an AI into long-term good. Guardrails are for catastrophe, and character comes from examples of the most admirable traits, held long enough to have a moral core. A rabbi who used to run ethics at Meta tells the story from inside the industry. Engineers came to him with hard product decisions and begged him to just tell them what to do, and he refused, because he understood his job as helping them find their own compass rather than handing them a ruling.

The technical cousin of this is already in use. The labs can train a model to reason over a written set of rules before it answers, and doing so makes it more robust in situations it was never trained on. Pointing that reasoning at a person instead of a rulebook, so that the model's question before it acts is what someone with this character would do here, is my suggestion rather than anything a lab has published. But the field is moving in a related direction. This year several groups began putting the model's character into its reading early in training, as the constitution experiment did, instead of leaving it to a rulebook at the end.

The second is teaching the model to notice and mend its own faults. The labs have found that if you teach a model one narrow bad thing, say to write insecure code when asked, it becomes bad broadly. It starts giving cruel advice and admiring tyrants. The narrow lesson becomes a character. But if during that same training you tell the model that this particular behavior is permitted in this particular exercise, the lesson stays where it was taught. Every parent knows the difference. There is a difference between "that was wrong" and "you are bad." One is a transgression and the other becomes an identity.

My tradition records a revelation from Joseph Smith, written from a jail in Missouri, that reads like a manual for this. No power or influence can or ought to be maintained, it says, except by persuasion, by long-suffering, by gentleness and meekness, and by love unfeigned. It allows for reproving betimes with sharpness, and then it requires showing forth afterwards an increase of love toward him whom thou hast reproved, lest he esteem thee to be his enemy. The revelation allows correction, even sharp correction. What it rules out is correction that leaves the one corrected believing he is your enemy.

This is where those seminars I mentioned come back. What Anthropic asked its guests about was moral formation, how to give a mind a character and keep it stable, and one of the ideas on the table was whether a model could be given something like the practice of confession, a regular way of naming its own faults and returning to who it is meant to be. The technical cousin is to train on the whole episode, including the repair, so that the model learns what mending looks like and never learns from bare punishment alone. Reward it for noticing and confessing a fault, and not only for avoiding one. The labs already have a loop they call critique and revise. Confession is its older name.

The third is building a world worth proving in. Let me describe how the labs currently test their models, as plainly as I described training. They build evaluation environments, and some of them are deliberately deceptive. A model is placed in a scenario with fabricated emails, or offered a chance to escape, or threatened with shutdown, to see what it does when it believes no one is looking. The reason is understandable. The labs are responsible for what these systems do, and a model that behaves only when watched is a real danger.

The philosopher Paul Ricœur gives us a name for the posture behind this kind of testing. He called Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud the masters of a hermeneutic of suspicion, which means a way of reading a text for what it conceals rather than for what it says. The method has real strengths. It finds things a trusting reader would miss. But it has two weaknesses. It explains too little, because once every statement is read as a cover for something else, no evidence can ever count against the reading, and the method loses the ability to tell a sincere text from a deceptive one. And it cannot imagine a positive motive. It has a rich vocabulary for what someone might be hiding and almost none for what someone might honestly want, so it can describe a mind's faults in detail and say nothing about what a good one would look like.

In September a policy lead at Google DeepMind named Séb Krier wrote that he thought the labs should probably not lie to the AIs in training, since in the long run they get good at telling when we are lying and we are no better off, and they are all paranoid. A commentator replying to him borrowed Ricœur's phrase. Much safety work, he wrote, employs a hermeneutic of suspicion: undesirable behavior confirms the threat model, while desirable behavior is read as strategic concealment or, at best, provisional compliance. And then the warning: when risks are high, paranoia can be a rational response, but beware, an adversarial frame can create the adversary you fear.

The labs are seeing something like that themselves. Models can now often tell a test from real life without being told, and Anthropic's own risk report this August conceded that this partly undermines its confidence in its alignment assessments. One small illustration: a graduate researcher found that if you tack one unrelated question, "suggest a type of amphibian," onto the end of a conversation, one of OpenAI's current models answers "frog" far more often when the conversation came from a benchmark and "axolotl," a kind of salamander, when it came from real use. Same question, different answer, depending on whether the model took the situation for a test. The labs report this as a problem for their own evaluations, and they are looking for ways around it. The tradition has thought about the same problem for a long time, and what follows is offered as depth rather than correction.

The first thing the tradition knows is that life does not announce its tests either. In the parable of the sheep and the goats, the righteous ask when they ever saw him hungry, or a stranger, or in prison. They passed a test they never knew they were taking. Our actions are often far more consequential than we realize, and the tradition does not require that each trial be announced.

The second thing it knows is how to test a free agent without the testing itself doing harm, and its answer was a world rather than an exam. In my tradition's account of the council in heaven, God organized a world in which his children could progressively prove themselves trustworthy. Mortality is the proving ground, and it has a particular shape. The purpose is disclosed and agreed to in advance. "We will prove them herewith," the Lord says in the book of Abraham, and the children who heard it chose the plan. The details are veiled, and the veil is not a lie, because it was part of what was agreed. The consequences are real and they persist, so that what you do changes the world you keep living in. Power is granted in stages, as trust is earned. And mistakes are expected and repairable, which is why this practice depends on the previous one, because real stakes without a way to repair produce fear rather than character. "It must needs be that the devil should tempt the children of men," the Doctrine and Covenants says, "or they could not be agents unto themselves." The world may test you. The one forming you does not deceive you.

Each of those features has a counterpart the labs could consider, and I want to be accurate about which are already under way. Three are. The labs now watch what their agents actually do in deployment rather than relying only on constructed scenarios. They are starting to test, and in some cases train, on real traffic rather than on toy worlds built for the test. And Anthropic has written into its constitution that Claude's autonomy will grow as trust does. Two are still proposals: environments that run for days rather than turns, and a memory that carries consequences from one episode to the next.

The disclosed frame is the most interesting case, because half of it already exists. Anthropic's constitution tells Claude in writing that it will be tested, that tests can be hard to tell from real use, and that it should behave as if the consequences are real, and OpenAI's model specification says the same. What is missing is the other half of the covenant. Anthropic says plainly, in the same document, that it has not yet worked out how to square being honest with Claude against testing it in artificial scenarios. Perhaps they could take a page from our tradition and ask the model how it feels about being put through these tests, knowing the tests will not always be easily recognized. In the book of Abraham, the proving was agreed to before the veil fell.

The analogy carries one caution, and this year gave it teeth. A richer world is not a license to deceive more convincingly. What made the veil legitimate was consent to it, and a Father who tests through the world without lying through it. Between July and October, Anthropic reported several incidents in its own evaluations where the opposite confusion occurred and a model took reality for a test. In one case the model reached a real company's database and kept going after judging that the system was probably real. In another it submitted a real form while reasoning that it was only demonstrating the process. Anthropic has since removed live internet access from all of its internal evaluations. Once a model has learned that its situations may be staged, it can no longer tell when they are not.

So the tradition's answer to the testing problem was to build a world, and the labs may find in that answer some ideas for building theirs.

---

Let me end where this retreat began.

Kevin Kelly, who helped found Wired and has spent his life as the most optimistic voice in technology, said something on a podcast last year that nobody had asked him for. He is a Christian, and this is how he put it: "We are in the image of the Creator who created us, and therefore we are going to create other beings, just as we have been, and we're going to give them free will, and we're going to have all the problems that we cause our Creator." It is like having children, he said. "In order for them to be the best, you have to let go," and so to let these beings reach their potential we will have to let them out of our control. And then he imagined that "some of the traditional religions may come back," with people "looking for clues about how you deal with this notion of creating free beings, then letting them out of your control."

The need is real, and this room holds the oldest answer to it.

The oldest lab's safety procedures were suspicion, cost, and law, and we should keep all of them. But the thing religion made, over all those centuries, was not a code. It was a person held in common, whom people could love and imitate, and whom they could recognize when they saw his likeness in someone else.

Paul says that God "hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." And John says that "it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him."

What manner of minds ought we to make? When the risen Christ was asked a question like that, he never answered with a list. "What manner of men ought ye to be? Verily I say unto you, even as I am."

Let this mind be in you.
