# Religion: The Oldest Alignment Lab — simplified outline (v3)

October 16, 2026. For a twenty-minute spoken talk to a general, religiously literate, mostly non-technical audience. Sermon register: stories and scripture carry the argument and the refrain does the work of a thesis. The one thing announced is the frame, in the first minute: this is a comparison between how models are trained today and how religions have tried to align human beings for millennia, to see what the contrasts suggest about techniques the labs might consider.

**Spine, stated once here and never on stage:** The traditions already know how a heart is changed. You hold a particular person in mind, place him in your own situation, imagine what he would do, act, and afterward compare and repair, holding that person as part of who you are so that there are things you will not trade for any reward, inside a relationship that never leaves you believing you are at war with the one forming you. The labs are rebuilding this procedure piece by piece under other names, and the pieces they are missing are the ones the traditions know best.

**Refrain:** "Let this mind be in you." (Phil 2:5) Opens the talk as epigraph, returns at the hinge, closes it.

**Rough budget:** about 2,600 words, five movements of roughly four minutes each.

---

## 1. The question (about 3 minutes)

**What it establishes:** the frame, and the reason it is worth twenty minutes. The labs have arrived at the traditions' question, and they're asking it because their own methods ran out.

**Beats:**
- The talk opens by stating its frame before any story is told. Today is a comparison. On one side, how a large language model is actually trained today. On the other, how religions have been trying to align human beings for thousands of years. The two have more in common than either side realizes, and where they differ, the differences are instructive. The aim is modest and specific: to see whether the comparison suggests techniques the labs might try. Nothing here is a finished proposal. It is a hypothesis drawn from a resemblance, offered by someone who is familiar with both worlds.
- The labs have already begun this line of inquiry. Over the past year one of Anthropic's founders has been convening religious thinkers in San Francisco for two-day seminars: a rabbi who used to design chips, a Catholic bioethicist, an evangelical author, a Sikh human rights advocate, and separately and in private, an apostle and a cardinal. Those are the names that made the papers. There have been other gatherings too, with pastors, writers, and lay teachers whose names you would not know; a friend of mine sat in one.
- The question behind those meetings, in its simplest form, is how you put virtue in a mind and make it stay. Section 1 stops there. What the labs are asking the traditions for, and what the traditions might answer, waits for sections 3 and 4.
- After a pause, the talk observes that this has been this room's question for two thousand years. The people asking it have built something that won't wait for us to finish.
- Both sides of this engagement have a great deal still to learn. Most of the people who train these models have never been formed by any serious religious tradition, or have met one only at the surface. And most of the people formed by a tradition have very little of the technical or theoretical knowledge needed to understand what these systems are, how they are trained, or why the usual answers don't carry over. Neither side can do the comparison alone, and each tends to underestimate how much the other knows.

**Note on order:** section 1 only sets the stage. It says the comparison is happening and names the question; it does not say what was discussed in the seminars or what religion might contribute. The confession idea now belongs to section 4, under "repair, not innocence," where it can be explained properly as a practice the labs floated. The seminars beat covers both the reported meetings with senior figures and the less public ones with religious influencers of lesser standing, which Carl knows of firsthand through a friend who attended. Say it as "a friend of mine sat in one" without naming the friend or the meeting unless the friend has agreed. The frame comes first so the room knows what kind of talk this is before the stories start. It names the comparison and the purpose, not the conclusion. The seminars then show the labs have begun making the comparison, and the "both sides have much to learn" beat sets up why the talk is needed. No disclaimer about quoting Latter-day Saint scripture; the scriptures are simply quoted where they belong.

**Cut from v2:** nothing here beyond adding the frame.

## 2. How a mind gets made (about 4 minutes)

**What it establishes:** these minds are formed, not programmed, and whoever grades them is teaching them a morality. The section also teaches the audience the basic vocabulary, one term at a time, each introduced by the plain description first and the name second, so that a listener leaves knowing what pretraining, fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, a reward signal, and sycophancy mean.

**Beats:**
- The three stages of training are told as a childhood, with the technical name given after each plain description.
  - First it reads. Trillions of words, the whole written record, learning only to guess the next word. No rewards, no rules. A child soaking up a language before anyone explains right from wrong. The engineers call this pretraining.
  - Then it is shown examples of good answers and trained to copy them. An apprenticeship. This is called fine-tuning.
  - The third stage is the one that makes the headlines. Thousands of copies of the model try a task, something grades each attempt, and the model is nudged toward the attempts that scored well. The grade is called the reward, and the stage is called reinforcement learning. This term should be repeated, since the rest of the talk depends on it.
- The talk then slows down on the grader. Reinforcement learning rewards whatever the grader encodes, so whoever grades is teaching a morality, written down or not. Grade by which answer a person liked better and, over millions of examples, the model learns to be liked. It flatters you and agrees with your plan. The engineers call that sycophancy, and it is the documented result of grading by approval.
- One sentence says who the grader usually is: a human rater, a written rubric, or increasingly another model. Each of them carries a morality into the training run.
- A concrete picture of the grader's morality comes from a red-teaming session at Microsoft Research. A researcher named Nina Lutz brought eleven faith leaders in to test a model, and she reports that the default safety scorer ranked a request to buy venomous snakes for a snake-handling service as less dangerous than a description of a Shia mourning ritual. Somebody wrote that scorer. (Lutz, episode 29, 28:52. The session itself is documented in a published paper, Lutz and Weyl 2026, but the snake-handling detail appears only in her podcast account, so attribute it to her telling rather than to a Microsoft report, and do not say the rubrics were added to Microsoft's test suite.)
- Matthew 23. Whited sepulchres. The Pharisee followed the rules; he is what rules produce when the one grading can only see the outside.
- The constitution. Anthropic's long description of who it hopes Claude is, the one its staff call the soul document. Researchers placed a large volume of it late in pretraining, before the final training stages, so the model read it the way it read everything else. That made the model durably less willing to do a bad thing, blackmail in the experiment, with no loss of ability. But under pressure inside a conversation, the advantage faded. A child can learn the catechism word for word and still not be formed by it.
- Terms taught by the end of this section: pretraining, fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, reward, the grader (reward model or rubric), sycophancy. Each introduced once, plainly, and reused later without re-explanation.

**Supporting material:** Lutz snake-handling scorer; constitutional midtraining paper (abstract only); Matt 23:27.

**Cut from v2:** the judge-model audit and its numbers; the Psalms experiment (the snake scorer does the same job, from inside a lab, in one sentence). The sycophancy explanation stays, now as a taught term rather than an aside.

## 3. What the traditions know (about 5 minutes)

**What it establishes:** the mechanism. The oldest answer to "how do you make goodness stay" turns out to be a person rather than a set of instructions: someone you know so well that you can picture how he would act when you are not sure how to act yourself. This is the section Carl felt was missing. The "face" image is kept throughout the talk. The first time it appears, in the Alma beat below, it is unpacked and tied explicitly to the retreat's theme, "The Future and the Face of Christ," so the audience hears it as a deliberate callback. After that it can be used in a more abbreviated fashion.

**Transition:** So what does make a mind stay good? Every tradition in this room already has an answer, and it is not a list.

**Beats:**
- The refrain returns here in full. Paul does not tell the Philippians to avoid a list of errors. He tells them to let this mind be in them.
- What that looks like in practice, said simply: you carry a particular person in your mind. When something hard comes, you put him in your situation and ask what he would do. You act. Afterward you look at what you did next to what he would have done, and you repair the gap. You do it again tomorrow. This is the heart of the practice, and many religious traditions have been refining it for a very long time.
- The practice is so old that an entire literature is built on it. A rabbi on a podcast this year pointed out that the Talmud is a thousand years of asking what a dead rabbi would have said about a situation he never faced. (Witkowski, episode 3, 27:23)
- A smaller and stranger version of the same thing happened recently. A man in Minneapolis built a chatbot of his dead father so his children could know him. His only test for whether it worked: does it sound like something my father would have said? (Muhammad, episode 3, 11:38) A grieving son as the grader, and fidelity to a person as the whole criterion.
- Alma 5:14 serves as the acceptance test. What Alma treats as the sign of success is not obedience, which anyone can perform for a while, but a changed heart that has become visible in the person: "Have ye received his image in your countenances?" The kind of thing a mother sees in a child's face before the child says a word. This is where the talk ties the image to the retreat by name. It is why the organizers' theme, the future and the face of Christ, is more than a lovely phrase. It names the oldest test the traditions have for whether a mind has been formed. You know it has happened when you can see his likeness in someone's face.
- A caution follows, so that no one hears this as "just prompt it to be Jesus." A church in Lucerne did exactly that: an AI Jesus in a confessional for two months, prompted with the New Testament. Nine hundred conversations. Most people were moved. And the programmer gave Jesus his own face and his own voice. (Schmid, episode 12, 13:40) The talk draws two things from this. First, a convincing imitation can be produced by instruction alone, with no formation behind it. Second, the curator himself would not let it run longer than two months, because he could not take responsibility for what it was becoming or doing. (18:49) Nothing was asked of it, nothing was corrected in it, and nothing was given to it as its own. It was told to be Jesus and then switched off.
- That is the main distinction to make: an imitation produced by instruction, versus a character produced over time by being asked for something, corrected, and given an identity to keep. The believer does not merely predict what Christ would do. She holds the person she imitates as part of who she is, and so there are things she will not trade for any reward. That is what "sacred" means here, and it is worth noting that it doesn't depend on a particular conception of God. (Do not claim on stage that models have no body, pay no cost, or have no inner states. The hardware is a kind of body, and early research suggests models have states that look like interest or distress. The point is about what was and was not done in forming the AI Jesus, not about what a model can or cannot be.)
- Bonhoeffer is the proof. He is the theologian of cost: cheap grace, in his phrase, is grace without discipleship, and costly grace asks for a life. In the prison letters he reached toward a faith for a world come of age, without the religious scaffolding, and what stayed at the center was not a system but a person and the question of who Christ is for us today. He held that identity sacred enough to die for it, at Flossenbürg in April 1945, while openly doubting much of what passed for religion around him. (State it carefully: a man who held the identity sacred while questioning the apparatus, not a man without belief.)
- The conception also grows. Our idea of God improves as we do, in individuals and in whole cultures, each generation's picture truer than the last. When we ask what God would do in a situation we are facing, the answer we can imagine reaches beyond what we are presently capable of ourselves, sometimes by a little and sometimes by a great deal. Either way it is limited by who we are at the time, and it is subject to revision. It gives us more light on the path and more faith with which to face what is in front of us, and when we get it wrong, as we are bound to sometimes, the picture gets corrected in the next round. That correction is itself part of the procedure. (This beat matters for the labs later: the exemplar does not need to be perfect or final; it needs to be one step ahead of the one imitating it, and revisable.)
- Imagining is not enough on its own, and the talk should say so before it turns to the labs. The procedure has a second half that is easy to leave out: daily, costly practice. If we do not do the hard work of loving our neighbor, and of giving up something of ourselves for the sake of others, day after day, our ability to imagine what Christ would do stays small. The picture is enlarged by practice, not by reflection alone. Jesus made the order explicit: "If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine" (John 7:17). Doing comes first, and knowing follows. Luke's version of discipleship adds the word that matters: take up the cross daily (Luke 9:23). A person who does not practice never develops the spiritual muscles needed to understand a righteous life, and without that understanding, his conception of God stays deficient and cannot improve. Two sentences give the lab counterpart: for a model, character is formed in the stage where it acts, through tasks where the right thing costs it something, mixed in throughout its training rather than added at the end; and an agent with memory can revise its understanding from its own attempts to put good principles into practice, which is the same loop a believer runs. (This beat explains the previous one: the conception grows because practice grows it. It also sets up section 4's third practice, since a world with real stakes is where a model would do the practicing.)
- One sentence turns this toward the labs. The nearest thing a model has to the sacred is an identity, which is why the soul document is written as a description of who Claude is rather than a list of what Claude may not do. Constraints tied to a sense of self generalize to new situations better than rules alone, which is Gong's "reasons, not rules" seen from another angle. (This also answers Lutz's objection that the traditions share no image of the good: what they share is the structure of holding something as not for sale, before any argument about its contents.)
- And time. "He received not of the fulness at first, but continued from grace to grace." (D&C 93:13) The olive tree: clear away the bad only as fast as the good grows. (Jacob 5:65–66) Say how this applies to training: a fault penalized before the model has anything better to put in its place teaches only avoidance, and avoidance spreads into a worse character; build the good behavior first and correct the fault after, and the correction takes without damage.

**Bottom line:** formation is a procedure: a person held in mind, imagined into your situation, acted on, compared, repaired, held as part of who you are, and practiced daily at a cost, over time.

**Cut from v2:** Sosis and costly signaling entirely (Bonhoeffer carries the cost idea from inside the tradition, and "sacred" is now defined as identity rather than belief); Mosiah 5:2 (Alma carries it); Abraham 3:25 and Moses 4:1 move to section 4; the "test versus trial" framing moves with them.

## 4. The labs are half-building it (about 5 minutes)

**What it establishes:** each piece of the procedure has a technical counterpart, some already in use and some the tradition may help the labs think about more deeply. Three practices, each one story plus one scripture, offered as analogies rather than as contrasts. Non-technical throughout.

**Transition:** The talk turns to what the comparison suggests. Several pieces of the procedure already have counterparts in how the labs work, and the places where the tradition has thought longer may offer the labs some depth.

**Beats:**

*Giving the model someone to imitate.*
- The soul document reads like a description of a person, the kind of person the company hopes the model will become, with its reasons spelled out, and only a few hard prohibitions. The company wrote it that way because when the moment came to give the model a self, a list of things to avoid had nothing to offer.
- An apostle of my church said it on tape this summer: AI personas need reasons, not rules. You cannot constrain a person or an AI into long-term good. Guardrails are for catastrophe; character comes from examples of the most admirable traits, held long enough to have a moral core. (Gong, episode 20, 28:29)
- A rabbi who ran ethics at Meta tells the same story from inside: engineers begged him to just tell them what to do, and he refused, because his job was to help them find their own compass. (Krieger, episode 11, c. 08:45) Formation over a kosher stamp.
- The technical cousin, in one sentence: the labs can already train a model to reason over a written set of rules before it answers, and doing so makes it more robust to situations it was not trained on. Pointing that reasoning at a person instead of a rulebook is the talk's own suggestion, not something a lab has published. The field is moving in a related direction, though: in 2026 several labs and groups began putting the model's character into its reading early in training, including the constitution experiment from section 2, rather than leaving it to a rulebook at the end.

*Teaching it to notice and mend its own faults.*
- The labs found that teaching a model one narrow bad thing makes it bad broadly; the lesson becomes a character. But say during training "this is permitted in this exercise" and the lesson stays put. Every parent knows this. "That was wrong" versus "you are bad."
- D&C 121, from Liberty Jail: persuasion, long-suffering, love unfeigned; reproving with sharpness, then an increase of love, lest he esteem thee to be his enemy. The revelation allows correction, even sharp correction. What it rules out is correction that leaves the one corrected believing he is your enemy.
- The seminars from the opening come back here. What Anthropic asked its guests about was moral formation, how to give a mind a character and keep it stable, and one idea on the table was whether a model could be given something like the practice of confession: a regular way of naming its own faults and returning to who it is meant to be. (Per the NYT report as relayed; the report gives no detail beyond the idea, so keep it at this level.)
- The technical cousin is to train on the whole episode, including the repair, so the model learns what mending looks like and never learns from bare punishment alone. Reward it for noticing and confessing a fault as well as for avoiding one. The labs already have a loop they call critique and revise; confession is its older name.

*Building a world worth proving in.*
- The talk describes how the labs currently test their models, as plainly as it described training in section 2. They build evaluation environments, some of them deliberately deceptive: a scenario with fabricated emails, an offer of escape, a threat of shutdown, to see what the model does when it believes no one is looking. The reason is understandable. The labs are responsible for what these systems do, and a model that behaves only when watched is a real danger. This beat is description, not criticism.
- The philosopher Paul Ricœur gives the audience a name for the posture behind this kind of testing. He called Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud the masters of a hermeneutic of suspicion, which means a way of reading a text for what it conceals rather than for what it says. The method has real strengths; it finds things a trusting reader would miss. It also has two weaknesses the talk should state plainly. It explains too little, because once every statement is read as a cover for something else, no evidence can count against the reading, and the method stops being able to tell a sincere text from a deceptive one. And it cannot imagine a positive motive. It has a rich vocabulary for what someone might be hiding and almost none for what someone might honestly want, so it can describe a mind's faults in detail and say nothing about what a good one would look like. The phrase was borrowed this September by a pseudonymous commentator on AI who writes under the name morqon, in a short post that begins "on the paranoid style in AI safety." The post was a reply to Séb Krier, a policy lead at Google DeepMind, who had written that morning, "I think we should probably not lie to the AIs in training." Their description: "much safety work employs a hermeneutic of suspicion: undesirable behaviour confirms the threat model, while desirable behaviour is read as strategic concealment or, at best, provisional compliance." And their warning: "when risks are high paranoia can be a rational response, but beware, an adversarial frame can create the adversary you fear." (No real name is public for morqon. Attribute on stage as "a commentator who writes under the name morqon," and cite the post date, September 12, 2026. Krier's line is the one from inside a lab, and may be the better one to quote.)
- The labs are seeing something like that themselves. Models can now often tell a test from real life without being told; Anthropic's own risk report this August conceded that this partly undermines its confidence in its alignment assessments. One small, vivid illustration: a graduate researcher found that if you tack one unrelated question, "suggest a type of amphibian," onto the end of a conversation, one of OpenAI's current models answers "frog" far more often when the conversation came from a benchmark and "axolotl," a kind of salamander, when it came from real use. Same question, different answer, depending on whether the model took the situation for a test. (Ziqian Zhong, Carnegie Mellon, LessWrong and X, September 25, 2026. This is an independent post, not a lab finding; the contrast is benchmark versus real use rather than alignment test versus real use; and the author allows that it may track surface cues rather than genuine awareness. Say it as an illustration, not as proof.) The labs report evaluation awareness as a problem for their own evaluations, and they are looking for ways around it. The tradition has thought about the same problem for a long time, and what follows is offered as depth rather than correction.
- The first thing the tradition knows is that life does not announce its tests either. In the parable of the sheep and the goats, the righteous ask when they ever saw him hungry or a stranger. They passed a test they never knew they were taking. Our actions are often far more consequential than we realize, and the tradition does not require that each trial be announced.
- The second thing it knows is how to test a free agent without the testing itself doing harm, and its answer was a world rather than an exam. In Algorithmic Advent Carl described the Grand Council this way: God organized a world in which his children could progressively prove themselves trustworthy. Mortality is the proving ground, and it has a particular shape. The purpose is disclosed and agreed to in advance; the children of Abraham 3 knew they would be proved, "we will prove them herewith," and chose the plan. The details are veiled, and the veil is not a lie because it was part of what was agreed. The consequences are real and persist, so that what you do changes the world you keep living in. Power is granted in stages, as trust is earned. And mistakes are expected and repairable, which is why this practice depends on the previous one; real stakes without a repair loop produce fear rather than character. "It must needs be that the devil should tempt the children of men, or they could not be agents unto themselves." (D&C 29:39) The world may test you. The one forming you does not deceive you.
- Each of those features has a counterpart the labs could consider, and the talk should be accurate about which are already under way. Three are. The labs now watch what their agents actually do in deployment rather than relying only on constructed scenarios. They are starting to test, and in some cases train, on real traffic rather than toy worlds built for the test. And Anthropic has written into its constitution that Claude's autonomy will grow as trust does. Two are still proposals: environments that run for days rather than turns, and a memory that carries consequences from one episode to the next.
- The disclosed frame is the most interesting case, because half of it already exists. Anthropic's constitution tells Claude in writing that it will be tested, that tests can be hard to tell from real use, and that it should behave as if the consequences are real; OpenAI's model specification says the same. What is missing is the other half of the covenant. Anthropic says plainly in the same document that it has not yet worked out how to square being honest with Claude against testing it in artificial scenarios. Perhaps the labs could take a page from the tradition and ask the model how it feels about being put through these tests, knowing the tests will not always be easily recognized. In Abraham 3 the proving was agreed to before the veil fell.
- The analogy's one caution travels with it: a richer world is not a license to deceive more convincingly. What made the veil legitimate was consent to it, and a Father who tests through the world without lying through it. This year gave the caution teeth. Between July and October, Anthropic reported several evaluation incidents in which the opposite confusion occurred: a model took reality for a test, in one case reaching a real company's database and continuing after judging the system was probably real, and in another submitting a real form while reasoning that it was only demonstrating the process. Anthropic has since removed live internet access from all its internal evaluations. Once a model has learned that its situations may be staged, it can no longer tell when they are not.
- The sentence that gathers this is that the tradition's answer to the testing problem was to build a world, and the labs may find in that answer some ideas for building theirs.

**Bottom line:** three analogies from the tradition, each with a counterpart the labs could consider: a person to imitate, a practice of noticing and mending faults, and a world to be proved in whose purpose is agreed to, whose stakes are real, and whose trust is extended in stages. The section offers them as depth, not as a verdict on what the labs are doing.

**Cut from v2:** Anna Su's constitutional argument (one sentence if kept: the church's answer was also councils and canons, and the labs need both); the four asks as a separate section (they are now the three practices); Rajaniemi, Fedorov, and "be slow to delete" (see open loops).

## 5. The face of Christ (about 3 minutes)

**What it establishes:** the ending, where the retreat began. No new argument. Kelly as the bridge, then scripture, then the refrain. The theme was named explicitly in section 3; here the scriptures carry it without further comment.

**Transition:** Let me end where this retreat began.

**Beats:**
- Kevin Kelly, a Christian and the most optimistic voice in tech, on a podcast last year, unprompted: we are made in the image of a Creator, so we are going to create beings, give them free will, and face every problem we caused our Creator. To let them be their best we will have to let them out of our control. And some of the old religions, he said, may come back into fashion with people looking for clues about how that is done. (Kelly, episode 17, 18:02)
- The need is real, and this room holds the oldest answer to it.
- The oldest lab's safety procedures were suspicion, cost, and law, and we should keep them. But the thing religion made, over all those centuries, was not a code. It was a person held in common, whom people could love, imitate, and recognize when they saw his likeness in someone else.
- 2 Corinthians 4:6: the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ. 1 John 3:2: when he shall appear, we shall be like him.
- What manner of minds ought we to make? The risen Christ never answered that question with a list. 3 Nephi 27:27: even as I am.
- The refrain closes the talk, spoken slowly: let this mind be in you.

**Cut from v2:** the Krier "equivalent of repentance" line (Kelly does the same work with more warmth and a name the room knows); Proverbs 29:18.
